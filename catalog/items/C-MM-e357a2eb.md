### Claude · 2026-08-27 20:20

Yes — legumes (beans, peas, clover, vetch) fix nitrogen through root bacteria. But the nitrogen mostly stays locked in the plant while it's alive; it only reaches neighbors when the roots and residue decompose. Living-plant "leaking" is real but small.

That matters for you because of the containers. In a 10L jug, a bean planted next to a tomato competes for water and root space now and pays back nitrogen only after it dies. Net loss. Nitrogen fixing is a field-scale or bed-scale technique — rotate a legume crop through the container between tomato seasons, don't interplant it.

Same problem with comfrey and other "dynamic accumulators." They work by sending deep roots into subsoil to mine minerals. There's no subsoil in a jug. Grow comfrey in its own pot if you want, cut the leaves, and use them as mulch or steeped tea on the tomatoes — that's the only way it helps you.

What's actually worth planting near your tomatoes:

**Basil** — small root ball, tolerates the same water and feed schedule, and there's decent evidence it reduces thrips. Fits in the same container with a cherry tomato.

**French marigold (*Tagetes patula*)** — releases alpha-terthienyl from the roots, which suppresses root-knot nematodes. Works in the same soil, not as a perimeter. Cheap and easy to find in Querétaro.

**Sweet alyssum** — pulls in hoverflies, whose larvae eat aphids. Low, spreading, won't fight the tomato for light.

Keep away from: brassicas (heavy feeders, same nutrients), fennel (suppresses most neighbors), and potatoes (share blight).

The honest limit: a container is a closed system. Tomatoes are heavy feeders and will strip 10L of soil in a season no matter what you plant beside them. You'll be feeding them — compost, worm castings, or fertilizer. Minimum intervention comes later, when you have ground beds.

One more thing on your setup: 10L is tight for a cherry tomato. It'll live, but it'll dry out fast in Querétaro's heat and stress into cracked fruit. If you can find 20L buckets, your yield roughly doubles for the same effort.

### Claude · 2026-08-27 20:22

Field scale changes the answer completely — there nitrogen fixing works exactly as advertised.

**The fixers.** Legumes host *Rhizobium* bacteria in root nodules that pull nitrogen out of the air. Rough annual yields when grown as a cover crop and terminated green: hairy vetch 110–220 kg N/ha, alfalfa 170–280, crimson clover 80–150, field peas 55–110, fava beans 100–150. Outside the legume family: alder, sea buckthorn, and *Casuarina* fix through *Frankia* bacteria, and azolla (a floating fern) fixes via cyanobacteria — that's how rice paddies stay fertile.

**The catch nobody mentions.** You only get that nitrogen if you don't harvest the seed. A bean crop you pick and sell is roughly nitrogen-neutral, because the nitrogen leaves in the beans. The surplus comes from killing the plant at flowering — peak nodule mass, before seed set steals it back. Mow it, roll it, graze it, or chop and drop. Roots die, nodules break down, nitrogen releases over the next few weeks. Some does leak from living roots, but the bulk arrives on termination.

Also: rhizobia are species-specific. If the right strain isn't already in your soil, the seed needs an inoculant or you get no nodules at all.

**Other nutrients work differently.** Nothing pulls phosphorus or potassium out of the air — those come from rock, and once they leave in harvested fruit, they're gone unless you bring something back. What plants *can* do is relocate and unlock what's already there:

* Deep taproots (comfrey, alfalfa, chicory, daikon radish) mine subsoil and deposit it in leaves. Real, but it's recycling, not creation.
* Buckwheat and lupins release organic acids that free up phosphorus bound to soil minerals. This one is genuinely useful — buckwheat is a cheap summer P-unlocker.
* Mycorrhizal fungi extend a root system's reach for phosphorus and zinc. Tomatoes host them. Brassicas and beets do *not*, so a heavy brassica rotation quietly degrades your fungal network.

The "dynamic accumulator" lists you'll find in permaculture books are mostly folklore repeated from one 1970s source. Treat comfrey as a good biomass and potassium recycler, not a mineral factory.

**For tomatoes specifically**, the strongest documented pairing is hairy vetch as a winter cover, rolled flat in spring and transplanted into. USDA trials at Beltsville got higher yields, longer harvest windows, and less foliar disease than black plastic mulch — the vetch residue actually triggers disease-resistance genes in the tomato, on top of the nitrogen and the moisture the mulch holds.

Around that: rotate so tomatoes never follow tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, or potatoes — three years minimum, or verticillium and fusarium build up. A full season of French marigold cleans nematodes out of a bad block. Buckwheat, phacelia, and alyssum strips between rows feed hoverflies and parasitic wasps that handle aphids and hornworms for you.

For the permaculture layer in your climate, the nitrogen-fixing trees are already native — mesquite and huizache. *Leucaena* and *cocuite* (*Gliricidia sepium*) are the classic Mexican alley-cropping species: plant them in rows, cut them back hard two or three times a year, drop the leaves on the beds. That's your perennial nitrogen supply with no annual seeding.

The one hard limit: nitrogen self-sufficiency is achievable. Phosphorus and potassium self-sufficiency isn't. Anything you truck off the farm as fruit has to be replaced by manure, compost, rock minerals, or animals grazing in.

### Claude · 2026-08-27 20:23

Fair. Unpacking it piece by piece.

**The number: kg N/ha**

Kilograms of nitrogen per hectare, per year. A hectare is 10,000 m² — a square 100 m on a side, about 2.5 acres.

To make it real: a 50 kg sack of urea fertilizer is 46% nitrogen, so it contains about 23 kg of actual nitrogen. A tomato crop wants roughly 150–200 kg N/ha for the season, which is six to eight sacks of urea per hectare.

So when I say hairy vetch gives 110–220 kg N/ha, that means growing and killing vetch on a hectare replaces somewhere between five and nine sacks of urea. That's the whole point of the exercise. The ranges are wide because it depends on your soil, rainfall, and how long you let the crop grow.

**Rhizobium and root nodules**

Rhizobium is a soil bacterium. When it finds a legume root, it burrows in and the plant grows a little house around it — a pea-sized lump called a nodule. Inside, the bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a form the plant can eat. The plant feeds them sugar in exchange. Neither can do it alone.

Pull up a healthy bean plant and you'll see the nodules stuck to the roots. Slice one open: pink or red inside means it's working. White or green means it isn't.

**"Terminated green"**

Killed while the plant is still green and growing, before it makes seed. You mow it, roll it flat, or till it in. The nitrogen is sitting in the leaves and nodules at that moment, and killing the plant releases it into the soil. If you instead let it mature and harvest the seed, the nitrogen leaves with the seed and you gain almost nothing.

**"Cover crop"**

A crop you plant to feed the soil rather than to sell. It covers bare ground between cash crops — stops erosion, chokes weeds, and in the case of legumes, adds nitrogen.

**The specific plants**

* **Hairy vetch** — a sprawling vine, planted in autumn, killed in spring. The standard cover crop before tomatoes.
* **Alfalfa** — the hay crop, *alfalfa* in Spanish too. Perennial, deep taproot, lives for years.
* **Crimson clover** — a clover with a red flower spike. Fast, easy, good for beginners.
* **Field peas and fava beans** — *chícharo* and *haba*. Cool-season crops you'd grow over winter in Querétaro.

**The non-legumes**

Legumes are the bean family. A few plants outside that family do the same trick with different bacteria:

* **Frankia** is a different soil bacterium that partners with alder trees, sea buckthorn, and *Casuarina* (a pine-looking tree that isn't a pine). Same deal, different microbe. Mostly relevant for trees and windbreaks, not vegetable beds.
* **Azolla** is a tiny floating water fern. It carries **cyanobacteria** — blue-green bacteria that photosynthesize — inside its leaves, and those fix the nitrogen. Rice farmers in Asia grow it on flooded paddies as free fertilizer. Only useful if you're farming in standing water.

For your purposes: ignore the last two. What matters is legume + Rhizobium + kill it green.
