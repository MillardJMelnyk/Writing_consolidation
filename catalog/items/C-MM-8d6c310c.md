what's the basis for the idea that Genesis 1 describes the *second* attempt of creation by YHWH?

### Claude · 2026-06-13 19:31

The "second attempt" framing draws on a cluster of textual observations, most associated with the **gap theory** or older **ruin-restoration** readings, but also on more mainstream critical scholarship. Here's what feeds it:

---

**1. Genesis 1:2 — "The earth *was* formless and void" (tohu wabohu)**

The Hebrew *hayah* ("was") can be read as "became" — i.e., the earth *became* waste, implying a prior state that deteriorated. Most critical scholars reject this as grammatically strained, but it's the cornerstone of gap theory readings (Scofield Bible popularized this in the 20th century). The argument: something happened *between* verse 1 and verse 2 that ruined the first creation.

---

**2. Isaiah 45:18 — "He did not create it tohu"**

God declares he didn't create the earth as *tohu* (formless waste) — yet Genesis 1:2 says it *was* tohu. The gap theorists use this as proof that the tohu state is a result of destruction, not original creation.

---

**3. Lucifer's fall as the intervening catastrophe**

The gap theory plugs Satan's primordial fall (Isaiah 14, Ezekiel 28, read as pre-Adamic history) into the gap between Gen 1:1 and 1:2, explaining why the earth was ruined. This is theology layered onto the textual gap, not exegesis of Genesis itself.

---

**4. The Enuma Elish / ANE parallels**

Secular critical scholarship approaches it differently: Genesis 1 looks like a *polemic rewrite* of Babylonian cosmogony (Marduk defeats Tiamat, creates from her corpse). The *tehom* ("deep") in Gen 1:2 is cognate with Tiamat. Some scholars read Gen 1 as consciously replacing an older mythological account — meaning the *cultural subtext* is a prior creation narrative being overwritten, not a second attempt by YHWH literally.

---

**5. The two creation accounts (Gen 1 vs. Gen 2)**

The Documentary Hypothesis (P source vs. J source) treats Gen 1:1–2:3 and Gen 2:4ff as two independent, contradictory creation accounts stitched together — different order of creation, different divine name (Elohim vs. YHWH Elohim), different literary style. This isn't "second attempt" theology but produces the same textual phenomenon: two starts to creation, with detectable seams.

---

**6. Ancient Jewish and Gnostic traditions**

Some early Jewish midrash (e.g., *Genesis Rabbah*) mentions that God created and destroyed multiple worlds before this one — "He created worlds and destroyed them" — before arriving at the current satisfactory creation. This is explicit prior-attempt theology, though not strictly tied to the Gen 1:1–1:2 gap.

---

**Bottom line:** The strongest *textual* basis is the tohu wabohu + Isaiah 45:18 tension plus the Gen 1/Gen 2 seam. The gap theory is the most systematic theological elaboration of it. Critical scholarship frames it as source-critical or ANE-influence issues rather than narrative theology about divine failure.

### Millard · 2026-06-13 19:36

what exactly is Genesis Rabbah?

### Claude · 2026-06-13 19:36

Rabbinic midrash — a verse-by-verse homiletical commentary on Genesis. Compiled roughly 400–500 CE in the Land of Israel, though it incorporates material from earlier tannaitic and amoraic periods.

"Midrash" means interpretive exposition — rabbis working through a biblical text by asking questions the plain reading raises, offering multiple answers, often contradictory ones sitting side by side without resolution. Genesis Rabbah is the major example of this genre applied to Genesis.

It's not a single author's work. It's a redacted anthology of rabbinic opinions, stories, wordplay, legal reasoning, and theological speculation attributed to various named sages across several generations. R. Abbahu (3rd c. Palestinian amora) is one of the named voices — the "created worlds and destroyed them" line is attributed to him specifically, in the section commenting on Gen 1:31 ("and God saw everything he had made and behold it was very good").

The argument there: *this* world pleased him — implying prior ones didn't.

It sits within normative rabbinic literature, not fringe or esoteric material. It's studied alongside Talmud in traditional Jewish learning. That's what gives it weight as a citation — you're not reaching for an obscure sect, you're citing mainstream classical rabbinics.

I always get confused between YHWH and Elohim (and I thing there's another one) when it comes to the Hebrew "god".

### Claude · 2026-06-13 19:49

The main ones:

**YHWH** (the Tetragrammaton) — the proper personal name. Four consonants, no vowels written. Pronunciation lost or suppressed; "Yahweh" is scholarly reconstruction, "Jehovah" is a medieval misreading. This is the covenant name — the name given to Moses at the burning bush, the name bound specifically to Israel's national deity. When you see "LORD" in all caps in English translations, this is YHWH.

**Elohim** — grammatically plural ("gods") used with singular verbs when referring to Israel's deity. Functions more as a title or category word than a proper name — means something like "god/gods/divine powers." When you see "God" in English translations, usually Elohim. The same word is used for foreign gods, for the divine council, and occasionally for human judges or powerful beings.

**El** — the root, and also a proper name. El was the chief deity of the Canaanite pantheon — ancient, patriarchal, presiding. Many scholars think early Israelite religion involved identifying YHWH *with* El, which is why the names compound: El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Olam — all epithets used for the patriarchs' deity in Genesis before the Sinai revelation of the YHWH name.

**Adonai** — means "my Lord/master." Not a name, a title. Substituted aloud for YHWH when reading — because the personal name became too sacred to pronounce, readers said Adonai instead. The vowels of Adonai got written under the consonants of YHWH in the Masoretic text, which is how "Jehovah" got invented (YHWH consonants + Adonai vowels, misread as one word).

**El Shaddai** — usually translated "God Almighty," actual etymology disputed. Possibly "god of the mountain," possibly something cruder. This is the name the patriarchs knew before Moses — Exodus 6:3 explicitly says YHWH wasn't known to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob by that name; they knew El Shaddai.

---

The Documentary Hypothesis rides heavily on this. The J source uses YHWH from the beginning (Gen 2 onward). The E source uses Elohim until the Sinai revelation. The P source is meticulous about not using YHWH before Exodus 6. This name-switching across Genesis is one of the primary seams the source critics use to carve up the text.

### Millard · 2026-06-13 19:50

what was the nature/character of the Canaanite El?

### Claude · 2026-06-13 19:50

El in the Canaanite pantheon as recoverable primarily from the Ugaritic texts (14th-13th c. BCE, discovered at Ras Shamra in 1929 — the richest source we have):

---

**Position**  
Supreme deity, father of the gods, head of the divine council. Not the most active or dramatic god — that's Baal — but the ultimate authority. Decisions required his approval. Other gods petitioned him.

**Character**  
Old. Explicitly depicted as aged, white-bearded, patriarchal. Called "Father of Years" (*ab shanim*), "Father of Humanity" (*ab adm*), "Bull El" — the bull being his symbol, connoting strength and fertility but also stolid, unhurried power. Not a storm god, not a warrior. Deliberate, enthroned, remote.

Benevolent and merciful in disposition. When petitioned he generally grants. He weeps, he feels, he isn't capricious. The texts show him as genuinely paternal — toward gods and humans both.

**Sexuality / fertility**  
He has consorts — Asherah primarily (also called Athirat), mother goddess, his wife. They produce the seventy gods of the pantheon together. El is sexually potent despite his age — there's a mythological text (*The Birth of the Gracious Gods*) depicting him impregnating two women on the shore, producing the minor deities Shachar and Shalem (dawn and dusk).

**Dwelling**  
Lives at the source of the two rivers, at the confluence of the deeps — a cosmic location at the edge of the world, often interpreted as where fresh and salt water meet. Remote, not locally present the way Baal is.

**Relationship to Baal**  
This is the tension in the Ugaritic texts. Baal is the young storm god, dynamic, warrior, dying-and-rising, agriculturally vital. He and El have an uneasy relationship — El isn't enthusiastic about Baal's ambitions but ultimately defers or is outmaneuvered. Some scholars read this as reflecting a historical religious shift where the storm-god cult was displacing the older patriarchal-El cult.

---

**Why this matters for Israelite religion**

The patriarchal narratives in Genesis map cleanly onto El-religion: El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Olam — all El epithets. The patriarchs' god is remote, makes covenants, promises land and descendants, is addressed as father. No storm, no warfare, no dragon-slaying.

YHWH in Exodus has Baal's attributes — storm, fire, warrior, mountain deity, defeats sea (Yam, whom Baal also defeats in the Ugaritic texts). The Sinai theophany is pure storm-god: thunder, lightning, smoke, earthquake.

The synthesis in Israelite religion is YHWH absorbing both: El's paternal covenant character and Baal's warrior-storm power, while insisting this is one deity not two, and purging the consort (Asherah) — though the archaeological evidence suggests Asherah worship persisted at the popular level well into the monarchic period. Inscriptions found at Kuntillet Ajrud read "YHWH and his Asherah" — which is either evidence of popular syncretism or evidence that the official monotheizing project was incomplete for a very long time.

### Millard · 2026-06-13 19:55

are there contexts where YHWH as "god" is not correct?

### Claude · 2026-06-13 19:55

Yes, several:

---

**YHWH as national/tribal deity, not universal god**

Much of the older textual stratum treats YHWH as Israel's god specifically — not the only god, but Israel's particular divine patron. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (Dead Sea Scrolls version, which differs from the Masoretic) reads: Elyon divided the nations among the sons of God, assigning each nation its deity — and YHWH's portion was Israel. That's a polytheistic or at minimum henotheistic frame, not monotheism. YHWH isn't "God" in that passage, he's one of multiple divine beings with a territorial assignment.

Judges 11:24 — Jephthah to the Ammonites: "Will you not possess what Chemosh your god gives you? So we will possess what YHWH our god gives us." That sentence only makes sense if Jephthah (and the text) is treating Chemosh as a real god with real jurisdiction. YHWH is Israel's god, Chemosh is Moab's god, parallel construction, no hierarchy implied.

**YHWH as one member of a divine council**

Psalm 82 — Elohim stands in the assembly of El, judges among the elohim. The other divine beings are real entities, they just rule unjustly and are condemned to die like men. YHWH (or Elohim here) isn't the only divine being, he's the one with legitimate authority among several.

Psalm 89:6-7 — "Who among the sons of the gods is like YHWH?" — comparative question only meaningful if other sons of gods exist as genuine comparanda.

Job 1-2 — the divine council scene where the sons of God (bene ha-elohim) present themselves before YHWH and the Adversary (ha-satan, not yet a proper name) is among them. YHWH is presiding over a populated divine court.

**YHWH as a being with a body**

Genesis — YHWH walks in the garden in the cool of the day, has a physical presence Adam and Eve hear approaching. Genesis 18 — YHWH appears as one of three men, eats food Abraham prepares, sits, converses. Exodus 33 — Moses can't see YHWH's face or he'll die, but YHWH covers Moses with his hand and lets him see his back. Isaiah 6 — the prophet sees YHWH seated on a throne, robe filling the temple, attended by seraphim.

"God" in the abstract philosophical sense — omnipresent, incorporeal, beyond perception — doesn't fit these passages. The being in them is located, embodied, perceptible, and has a face and a back.

**YHWH as ignorant or uncertain**

Genesis 3 — YHWH asks Adam "where are you?" Genesis 18 — YHWH says he's going to Sodom to see whether the report about it is accurate: "I will go down and see whether they have done altogether according to the outcry that has come to me. And if not, I will know." An omniscient being doesn't go investigate. He doesn't know yet.

**YHWH as emotionally reactive and changeable**

Exodus 32 — YHWH intends to destroy Israel after the golden calf; Moses argues him out of it and "YHWH relented of the disaster he had spoken of bringing on his people." A being who relents in response to argument isn't operating from eternal immutable will.

Numbers 14, Amos 7, Jonah — same pattern. YHWH announces destruction, is interceded with, changes course. The Hebrew word is *nacham* — relented, repented, changed his mind.

---

**The bottom line**

"God" as the word functions in post-Enlightenment theology — omniscient, omnipotent, omnipresent, incorporeal, morally perfect, universal — is a Hellenistic philosophical construct that got retrofitted onto YHWH, mostly through Philo of Alexandria and then the church fathers. The YHWH of the older textual layers is a being with a body, a territory, a people, a council of peers, incomplete knowledge, and a temper he can be talked out of. Calling him "God" in the universal sense reads centuries of philosophical theology back into texts that don't support it.

### Millard · 2026-06-13 19:56

so El and YHWH are not the same, or sometimes yes and sometimes no?

### Claude · 2026-06-13 19:56

Both, depending on which layer of the text and which period of Israelite religion you're looking at.

---

**Originally: distinct**

El is the Canaanite patriarch-deity, head of the pantheon, father of the gods. YHWH appears to have separate origins — possibly a deity of the southern wilderness, associated with the Midianites or Kenites (the "Kenite hypothesis"). Exodus has Moses encounter YHWH for the first time through his Midianite father-in-law Jethro, who is a priest — of what deity isn't said, but the geography and the priestly role are suggestive. YHWH's earliest poetic appearances (Judges 5, Deuteronomy 33) place his origin at Seir, Edom, Sinai, Paran — southern wilderness, not Canaan proper.

So the oldest recoverable picture: two distinct deities, different origins, different character, different geography.

---

**Then: deliberately merged**

As Israelite religion developed, YHWH absorbed El. The mechanism was probably both political and theological — the claim that the god the patriarchs worshipped as El Shaddai/El Elyon *was* YHWH all along, they just didn't know his name yet. Exodus 6:3 makes this explicit as a retrospective identification. The merger gave YHWH El's attributes — the paternal covenant-making, the antiquity, the title "father," the creator role — while El as a separate deity disappeared from Israelite religion.

---

**The result: textual layering**

Different passages reflect different moments in that merger process. Some texts treat them as identical (the monotheizing editorial layer). Some texts preserve traces of distinction — YHWH as one of El's sons, El Elyon as a separate referent above YHWH. You can't read the Tanakh as a consistent theological document on this question because it isn't one — it's a centuries-long accumulation of texts from different periods, different priestly traditions, different stages of the merger, imperfectly harmonized.

---

The short answer: historically distinct, theologically merged, textually inconsistent.

- Ezekiel: economic injustice and neglect of the poor  
- Rabbinic tradition: violation of hospitality as a fundamental social covenant

The homosexuality reading is probably the least supported by the full textual picture and the most motivated by later moral agendas.

The accusations include child sacrifice — burning children to Moloch/Molech — but cannibalism specifically isn't a prominent charge in the Tanakh against Canaanites.

If someone is using cannibalism as part of the Canaanite justification narrative today, they're likely drawing on later Christian or medieval elaborations, not the Tanakh itself.
