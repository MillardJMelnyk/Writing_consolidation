### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:01

Speciation is the process by which one species splits into two or more distinct species — populations diverge until they can no longer interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 16:16

The example you gave me seems tenuous in contrast to the level of complexity involved in what is usually referred to as speciation

### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:16

You're right that polyploid speciation is the cheap case. Chromosome doubling in one generation gives instant reproductive isolation without any of the machinery usually meant by speciation — divergent selection, geographic separation, accumulation of incompatibilities, gradual loss of interfertility.

Direct observation of that harder case is thin. The strongest candidates: apple maggot flies (*Rhagoletis pomonella*) shifting from hawthorn to apple after 1864 and now showing partial reproductive isolation — partial, not complete. Sockeye salmon in Lake Washington diverging into beach and river spawners in about 13 generations. Darwin's finches on Daphne Major, tracked since 1973, showing a lineage isolated by song and beak morphology. All of these are speciation in progress, not completed. The honest position is that the complete process on the standard model takes longer than the history of observation, so what's directly observed is either the shortcut mechanism or the early stages of the long one.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 16:19

There is a level of complexity presumed when people talk about speciation. What's your best guess at the threshold below which it would only be called speciation because it resembles what has already been designated as speciation.

### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:19

My best guess at the threshold: reproductive isolation that depends on a single locus, a single chromosomal event, or an environmental condition — as opposed to intrinsic, multi-locus, genome-wide incompatibility that persists when you put the two populations in a jar together.

Everything below that line gets called speciation by resemblance. The tell is reversibility. Lake Victoria cichlids that were treated as good species fused back into hybrid swarms when eutrophication killed the water clarity their mate-choice depended on. That isolation was one sensory cue deep. Same logic for the apple maggot flies: isolation is host preference plus timing, and if the apples disappear so does the barrier. Polyploids are the opposite failure — the barrier is real and instant, but it skips the entire process people mean by the word.

What the standard model actually presumes, when people say speciation without qualification, is Dobzhansky-Muller incompatibilities: alleles that accumulated separately in two lineages and are individually harmless but broken in combination. That requires many loci, sustained separation, and enough time that the genomes have drifted into mutual disagreement. Nobody has watched that happen from start to finish. Hybrid sterility or inviability arising *de novo* under observation is, as far as I know, unobserved outside of polyploidy and a handful of chromosomal rearrangements in the lab.

So the honest split is three tiers, not one word: instant isolation by genomic accident, conditional isolation by ecology or behavior, and intrinsic isolation by accumulated incompatibility. Only the third is the thing the term was built to describe. The first two are inferred to be its early stages or its shortcuts, and that inference is the load-bearing assumption, not an observation.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 16:20

And what's the reason for setting the threshold there?

### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:20

Because that's where the barrier stops depending on circumstances the organisms didn't create and can't carry with them. Intrinsic incompatibility travels with the genome — move the two populations anywhere, remove the host plant, restore the water clarity, and they still can't produce fertile offspring. Everything below that line is contingent on a condition that can be removed, and when it's removed the populations merge again. That's the operational reason: it's the only version of the barrier that's a property of the lineages rather than of their situation.

The theoretical reason is that the biological species concept defines species by reproductive isolation, and if the isolation is one cue or one locus deep, you've defined a species by something that can be undone in a season. That makes the category unstable in a way the concept wasn't meant to tolerate.

But I should be straight about what kind of claim this is. That threshold is a judgment about where the word stops meaning what people think it means, not a line drawn in the literature. Working biologists mostly don't set one — speciation is treated as a continuum of divergence, and the argument over where along it to plant a flag is old and unsettled. Mayr would have put it near where I did. Ecological-speciation researchers would say I'm privileging intrinsic barriers for no principled reason, since extrinsic ones do the same work as long as the environment persists, and environments persist for millions of years. Anyone using a phylogenetic species concept doesn't need reproductive isolation at all — diagnosable monophyly is enough, and my threshold is irrelevant to them.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 16:31

Okay, back to what a realistic, practical, and useful threshold would be

### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:31

Practical threshold: hybrids are produced but don't propagate — either they're sterile, inviable, or systematically outcompeted — so gene flow between the two populations is effectively zero even where they overlap.

That's the useful line because it's what you can measure. You put them together, you count hybrids, you check whether hybrid offspring carry alleles into the next generation. If they don't, the two gene pools are independent from that point forward and everything downstream — divergence, adaptation, further splitting — proceeds separately. That's the consequence people actually care about when they use the word.

It doesn't require you to know the genetic architecture, or to decide whether the barrier is intrinsic or ecological, or to predict whether it lasts. It's a state you can test for.

The known cost: it's only testable where the populations can be put in contact, and for allopatric populations you're estimating what would happen rather than observing it. That's the standard problem with the biological species concept and this threshold inherits it.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 16:34

### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:34

Statement: gene flow between the two populations is effectively zero.

Gene flow means alleles from one population ending up in the other and staying there across generations. Effectively zero means the rate is low enough that the two gene pools change independently — selection and drift in one no longer move the other.

That is compatible with hybrids being born, and even with hybrids reproducing, as long as their descendants don't carry alleles back into either parent pool in numbers that matter. It's a measurement of what crosses the boundary, not of whether individuals cross it.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 16:42

Okay define alleles

### Claude · 2026-09-01 16:42

An allele is one of the alternative versions of a gene that can occupy the same spot on a chromosome.

Every individual carries two copies of each gene, one from each parent, and those copies can be the same version or different versions. A population's gene pool is the full set of alleles present across all its members, in whatever proportions.

### Millard · 2026-09-01 20:10

Somehow that seems basic ins general. Some alleles would be relevant to speciation, but most would not.

### Claude · 2026-09-01 20:10

No — for the threshold, every allele counts equally.

The measurement isn't about which alleles matter functionally. It's about whether anything crosses. Neutral alleles are the better test precisely because they're not under selection: if a neutral allele arises in one population and shows up in the other, gene flow exists, and it drags whatever else it's linked to with it. Alleles that matter to speciation — the ones underlying isolation — are the ones least likely to cross, so they're the worst indicator of whether the boundary is open.

That's how it's actually measured. Neutral markers across the genome, checked for shared variation that can only be explained by recent exchange.

### Claude · 2026-09-01 20:19

Don't take me as the field's best thinking — I fumbled this badly enough that you shouldn't generalize from it.

But the underlying problem is real and it has a name: the species problem. There are dozens of competing species concepts in the literature, no agreement on which is correct, and the disagreement is about definition, not data. Where the concept holds up is at the ends: lineages that clearly can't interbreed, and populations that clearly do. Where it doesn't is the middle — and the middle is where nearly every contested case sits.

So the practical split: claims that a lineage split and the descendants stopped exchanging genes are solid. Claims that population A and population B are two species rather than one carry all the fuzziness, and often reduce to which concept the author picked.
