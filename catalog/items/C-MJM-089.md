### Claude · 2025-07-20 17:45

You're referring to Jeremiah 17:9: "The heart is deceitful above all things and beyond cure. Who can understand it?" This is indeed a striking statement about human nature.

The relationship between this Old Testament perspective and New Testament teachings is more nuanced than directly contradictory. Here's how they relate:

**Areas of apparent tension:**
The New Testament does emphasize transformation of the heart - Jesus speaks of pure hearts being blessed, and Paul writes about God giving believers new hearts. This seems to contrast with Jeremiah's pessimistic assessment.

**Areas of continuity:**
However, the New Testament doesn't actually reject the diagnosis of the human heart's corruption. Jesus himself said "out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery" (Matthew 15:19), and Paul extensively describes human moral corruption in Romans. The New Testament agrees that the natural human heart is problematic.

**The resolution:**
The key difference isn't in the diagnosis but in the solution. The Old Testament, including Jeremiah, often points toward a future hope - Jeremiah himself prophesies about God giving people new hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). The New Testament presents this transformation as now available through Christ and the Holy Spirit.

So rather than contradicting Jeremiah's assessment, the New Testament builds on it - acknowledging the heart's deceitfulness while offering the promised remedy of spiritual transformation. Both testaments share the view that humans need divine intervention to have trustworthy hearts.

### Millard · 2025-07-20 17:45

Compare the Hebrew word translated "heart" to the Greek word from Jesus' statement.

### Claude · 2025-07-20 17:46

Great question! The Hebrew and Greek words for "heart" have fascinating similarities and subtle differences:

**Hebrew - לֵב (leb/lev):**
In Jeremiah 17:9, the Hebrew uses "leb" (or the variant "lebab"). The Hebrew word encompasses "the heart, the feelings, the will, the intellect, centre" - essentially the entire inner person. In Hebrew thinking, "the heart is the 'kernel of the nut;' the internal citadel of the soul" and represents "the central citadel of a man's personality."

**Greek - καρδία (kardia):**
In Matthew 15:19, Jesus uses the Greek "kardia." This word means "the heart, i.e. (figuratively) the thoughts or feelings (mind)" and has a broader semantic range including both emotional and intellectual aspects.

**Key similarities:**
Both Hebrew "leb" and Greek "kardia" refer to the totality of the inner person - not just emotions, but the seat of thought, will, moral decision-making, and personality. Both testaments use "heart" as "the word for the inner self" appearing "just under 1,000 times" throughout Scripture.

**The theological continuity:**
This linguistic analysis actually strengthens the theological continuity I mentioned earlier. Both Jeremiah and Jesus are talking about the same comprehensive reality - the core of human personhood that includes intellect, emotions, will, and moral capacity. Neither is speaking merely about feelings, but about the fundamental center of human nature that both testaments agree is corrupted and in need of divine transformation.

The words are functionally equivalent in their scope and meaning.
