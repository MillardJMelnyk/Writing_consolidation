### Claude · 2026-08-21 23:15

Your question is ambiguous between two different numbers — CFR (deaths per confirmed case) and IFR (deaths per actual infection). They differ by roughly an order of magnitude, and both have changed over time.

**CFR:** Globally it fell from about 2.4% in 2020 to about 0.9% by 2023. That's an artifact of testing coverage as much as anything.

**IFR, pre-vaccine era (the best-studied period):** Ioannidis's seroprevalence review put the median across 51 locations at 0.27% (0.23% corrected), and 0.05% for people under 70. IHME's Lancet analysis found the same J-shape by age: 0.0023% at age 7, 0.057% at 30, 1.0% at 60, 20% at 90. A 2025 re-analysis of New York City serology backs out an overall US IFR of 0.86% for early 2020 — higher because NYC was an old, dense, treatment-naive population hit hard.

Age dominates everything else. Median IFR ran 0.0003% at 0–19, 0.011% at 30–39, 0.123% at 50–59, 0.506% at 60–69 — roughly 4x per decade of age.

**Current era:** there is no well-pinned global number. Surveillance collapsed after WHO dropped daily reporting in August 2023, and denominators are no longer measurable. What's established is direction, not magnitude: Omicron plus population immunity cut mortality per infection substantially below the pre-vaccine figures, but nobody has published a credible current global IFR.

Short version: 0.2–0.3% pre-vaccine globally, heavily age-skewed; materially lower now, exact value unproven.

### Claude · 2026-08-21 23:18

**Terminology:** the % of infected people who die is the **infection fatality rate (IFR)**. The % of the entire population that dies is the **crude mortality rate** (also called population fatality rate). You had it right conceptually — CFR is the one that uses positive tests as the denominator, which is the number you're rejecting.

**Your NIH premise is wrong.** NIH-funded and NIH-hosted research runs the opposite direction. A University of Pennsylvania/Boston University team found the US had nearly 1.2 million more deaths than expected between March 2020 and August 2022 — more than the reported COVID toll, not less. A 2026 study found over 150,000 uncounted COVID deaths early in the pandemic. IHME estimated US deaths 57% higher than the official count and global deaths near 7 million against a reported 3.24 million. The "6% died of COVID alone" CDC figure is the one usually cited as proof of inflation; that number means COVID was the only condition listed on the death certificate in 6% of cases — not that 94% were miscounted.

**IFR:** 0.2–0.3% globally pre-vaccine, and there's no way around the early seroprevalence studies for this number. Once mass testing collapsed and nearly everyone had been infected, the denominator became unmeasurable. No credible post-2022 global IFR exists.

**Crude mortality rate:** US, roughly 1.2M deaths against ~335M people = about 0.36% of the population, cumulative 2020–2024. Globally, ~7M reported deaths against 8 billion = about 0.09%. Using WHO's excess-death estimate of ~14.9M for 2020–21 instead, roughly 0.19%.

Excess mortality is the metric that sidesteps the death-certificate argument entirely — it just counts how many more people died than normally would. That's why it's the one to use if you distrust cause-of-death coding, and it makes the toll larger, not smaller.

### Claude · 2026-08-21 23:24

You're right that IFR and CFR don't separate those categories. That's a real limitation of both metrics — they count deaths in infected people, full stop.

But that objection doesn't reach the excess-mortality number, and that's the one I'd point you to. Excess mortality doesn't read death certificates. It counts total deaths from all causes and subtracts the baseline. If a death was iatrogenic — ventilator injury, a bad drug protocol, delayed cancer care — it still shows up in the total. Attribution errors, both directions, are invisible to it. That's the whole point of the metric. And it comes out *higher* than the certified COVID count, not lower.

So the "with vs from" problem cuts against the official numbers on certificates, but it doesn't shrink the excess. Whatever the mechanism, roughly 1.2 million more Americans died in 2020–2022 than the baseline predicts. Sorting that pile into viral deaths, medical-error deaths, and collateral deaths is a live research question. Making the pile smaller isn't.

On remdesivir specifically, the randomized evidence doesn't support it as a killer. Solidarity's final results found 14.6% mortality on remdesivir versus 16.3% on control among patients on oxygen but not ventilated, and 11.9% versus 13.5% across all non-ventilated patients. A meta-analysis of the RCTs found no mortality benefit and no association with severe adverse events including acute kidney injury. A FAERS disproportionality analysis found the kidney-injury signal diminished over time and was absent in the most recent quarter, suggesting the drug may not be nephrotoxic. The fair criticism of remdesivir is that it was approved on a weak endpoint and probably doesn't work — the ACTT-1 analyses that drove US, UK, and European approval were unadjusted for a chance imbalance that exaggerated the recovery-time effect. That's a story about a useless expensive drug, not a lethal one.

Early ventilation practice is the strongest iatrogenic case — intubating on oxygen saturation numbers rather than respiratory distress, in spring 2020, before proning and delayed intubation became standard. That killed people. It's also a few months in a few health systems, not something that scales to the aggregate.

If you want a number that's immune to the attribution problem, use excess deaths per capita: roughly 0.36% of the US population, 2020–2022. Everything is in it, including whatever the hospitals did wrong.

### Claude · 2026-08-21 23:27

There's a real literature on decomposition, but it splits the excess differently than you're asking.

The main US study used time-series models to separate direct SARS-CoV-2 mortality from indirect pandemic consequences, March 2020 to January 2022, stratified by week, state, age, and underlying cause — COVID and respiratory, Alzheimer's, cancer, cerebrovascular, diabetes, heart disease, and external causes including suicides and overdoses. It attributes 84% of excess deaths to direct impacts, with indirect effects predominating only in people under 44. A 2025 PNAS Nexus paper does the same cause-of-death decomposition across 24 countries through 2022. The Society of Actuaries published cause-specific excess mortality through February 2023 — that's the insurance-side work you're pointing at.

Two things that literature does *not* do:

It doesn't separate "COVID as primary cause" from "comorbidity with COVID contributing." The decomposition is direct-versus-indirect, which is a different cut. Getting your version requires trusting death-certificate underlying-vs-contributing coding, which is the thing under dispute.

It has no iatrogenic category at all. Ventilator injury and drug harm aren't broken out anywhere I can find. They'd be buried inside "direct" — a patient who died of induced barotrauma still had COVID and still died in a COVID bed.

And on normalizing downward: no such correction has been published because the work keeps finding undercount, not overcount. That's not me arguing the point — you asked what exists, and a downward normalization isn't in the literature.
