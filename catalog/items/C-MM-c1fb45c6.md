### Millard · 2026-10-01 12:25

Socrates idea of amathia: did he see this as a generalized social phenomenon or was it more of an individual defect?

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:25

An individual defect of the soul (believing you know what you don't, especially about the good) that he found in nearly everyone he questioned, politicians, poets and craftsmen alike, and treated as the root of Athens' public failures. The explicit framing of amathia as a state-level disease comes later, in Plato's *Laws*, from the Athenian Stranger rather than Socrates.

### Millard · 2026-10-01 12:27

Did he have any clue that it's just a symptom of codependence induced cultism?

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:27

Not in those terms, but the closest he comes is in Plato's *Republic* VI, where Socrates calls the crowd itself the greatest sophist, shaping people's convictions through collective praise and blame until they mistake the herd's approval for knowledge of the good.

### Millard · 2026-10-01 12:30

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:30

Mostly, yes: in the *Apology* and *Alcibiades I* amathia is the cause of wrongdoing and political error. Have you considered that *Republic* VI runs the other way, with the crowd's approval-and-blame producing the amathia, which puts it closer to your symptom framing?

### Millard · 2026-10-01 12:31

No, I'm not that familiar with the Republic

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:31

The relevant passage is short: Socrates says that when people gather in assemblies, courts and theaters, their roaring praise and blame floods a young person's mind until he adopts the crowd's values as his own, and the professional sophists merely study the moods of this "great beast" and call whatever pleases it good. So in that passage the amathia is manufactured by group approval-seeking, which is the mechanism your codependence framing describes.

### Millard · 2026-10-01 12:33

Sounds like it. Accounts for the softests (propagandists and narrative manipulators). What's missing from that are the cult leaders who orchestrate the whole thing in in service of their interests.

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:33

Plato covers that figure, just not in Book VI: in Book I, Thrasymachus argues that rulers write the laws and define "justice" to serve their own advantage, and in Book VIII the popular champion who rides the crowd's appetites into power becomes the tyrant. The awkward part is that Plato's own fix, the "noble lie" in Book III, has his philosopher-rulers orchestrating a founding myth for the public's good, which is the same mechanism with the claim of better intentions.

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:37

Karl Popper made the Plato half of that case in *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, calling Plato's republic a blueprint for totalitarianism, and I.F. Have you considered that Popper went the other way on Socrates, casting him as the open-society individualist whom Plato betrayed, and how that split bears on your "lesser degree" placement?

### Millard · 2026-10-01 12:38

### Claude · 2026-10-01 12:39

Popper's split rests on separating the dialogues: he took the early ones (*Apology*, *Crito*) as the real Socrates, whose admission of ignorance and relentless questioning of authority made him the model of the critical, open mind, and treated the *Republic* as Plato putting his own authoritarian program in Socrates' mouth. Stone reads the same early material and finds a man who saw ordinary citizens as unfit to govern and defended free inquiry for himself rather than for everyone.
