### Claude · 2026-08-07 18:40

Structures that pair naturally with inversion in review-writing, C2 level:

**Cleft sentences** — isolate the thing you're praising/criticizing.  
*"What makes the film remarkable is its restraint." / "It is the silence, not the dialogue, that carries the film."*

**Participle clauses** (present/past) — compress description, avoid choppy short sentences.  
*"Shot almost entirely in natural light, the film never feels staged." / "Having established its world in ten wordless minutes, the story trusts the viewer completely."*

**Non-defining relative clauses** — add critical commentary without a new sentence.  
*"The final act, which many will find too slow, is where the film earns its title."*

**Nominalization** — turns verbs/adjectives into abstract nouns, raises register.  
*"The director's refusal to explain herself" instead of "she refuses to explain."*

**Comparative/superlative structures with qualifiers** — standard in critical prose.  
*"Arguably the most assured debut of the year." / "Nothing short of remarkable."*

**Absolute constructions** — for scene-setting, very literary.  
*"Its plot stripped to the bone, the film relies entirely on performance."*

**Tricolon / parallelism** — rhythm, persuasive weight, pairs well with inversion for a strong close.  
*"Quiet, exact, and unforgettable."*

**Concessive structures (Although/While/Despite)** — needed for balanced critique.  
*"While the pacing falters mid-film, the payoff justifies the patience."*

**Emphatic do** — reinforces claims, often right after an inversion.  
*"Rarely does a debut announce itself so confidently. It does so without a single wasted shot."*

**Hedged evaluation (modals + adverbs)** — softens absolute claims, sounds more critic-like than student-like.  
*"This may well be the year's most overlooked film."*

For homework: have students rewrite 3–4 sentences of the model review by adding one of these structures to each inverted sentence — e.g., turn "Rarely have I left a cinema so certain" into a cleft, or attach a participle clause to "No sooner had the opening scene faded."

'to those allies ho align ith our interests." hat structure is this grammaticaly?

### Claude · 2026-08-07 19:06

That's not inversion — it's a **fronted prepositional phrase** with a **defining relative clause** inside it.

Breakdown:  
- **"To those allies"** — prepositional phrase, moved to the front of the sentence for emphasis/topic-focus (this is "fronting," not inversion — the main clause after it keeps normal subject-verb order, e.g. "To those allies who align with our interests, **we pledge** our support").  
- **"who align with our interests"** — defining relative clause, modifying "allies," identifying which allies you mean.

This is the exact pattern JFK uses repeatedly in the inaugural: *"To those old allies whose cultural and spiritual origins we share, we pledge the loyalty of faithful friends."* Same structure — fronted "To + noun phrase" + relative clause, then the main clause.

No auxiliary/subject inversion here — that's the key difference from what you've been teaching all class. If you wanted to make it a true inversion, you'd need a negative/restrictive adverbial in front position with subject-aux inversion, not a plain prepositional phrase.

### Millard · 2026-08-07 19:07

to the tiger, devoured shall be. Is this correct?

### Claude · 2026-08-07 19:07

No — that's not grammatical, and it's not one of the inversion structures from today's class.

Problems:  
1. **No subject.** "Devoured shall be" has no stated subject — who or what gets devoured? Compare to real inversion, which always keeps the subject, just moves it after the auxiliary: "Never has such cruelty been witnessed" — subject "such cruelty" is still there, after "has."  
2. **Wrong preposition.** You devour something, or something is fed *to* the tiger — you don't devour something *to* a tiger. "To the tiger" doesn't attach correctly to "devoured."  
3. **This isn't adverbial inversion at all.** There's no negative/restrictive adverbial (never, rarely, only, not, no) triggering it. Fronting a prepositional phrase + reversing verb/auxiliary order like this is just archaic/poetic word-scrambling (it reads like Yoda), not the grammatical structure you're teaching.

If you want a real inversion with this content, something like:  
**"Never has a creature been devoured so mercilessly by the tiger."**  
or, fronting "to the tiger" correctly with a passive:  
**"To the tiger the goat was fed."** (this is fronting + normal SVO, not inversion — no auxiliary/subject swap, since there's no negative adverbial triggering it)

Worth using as a counterexample in class — show students what happens when they force inversion without a trigger word.

### Millard · 2026-08-07 19:08

those who foulishly sought poer, hat structure is this?

### Claude · 2026-08-07 19:08

**"Those who foolishly sought power"** — a **noun phrase with a defining relative clause**.

Breakdown:  
- **"Those"** — head noun (a pronoun meaning "the people/ones").  
- **"who foolishly sought power"** — defining relative clause, modifying "those," identifying which people you mean (the ones who sought power foolishly, not some other group).  
- **"foolishly"** — adverb inside the relative clause, modifying "sought."

This is the JFK line in full: *"those who foolishly sought power by riding the back of the tiger ended up inside."* The whole phrase "Those who foolishly sought power by riding the back of the tiger" is the subject of the sentence; "ended up inside" is the main verb phrase.

Same structure as "the man who called yesterday," "the students who passed the exam" — standard defining relative clause, nothing exotic. No inversion, no participle clause, no cleft. Worth having students identify what it is NOT, since they're currently pattern-matching everything in the speech to the structures from today's lesson.
