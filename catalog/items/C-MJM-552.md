### Millard · 2025-12-31 15:35

Paul often referred to "the Lord", e.g., 1 Corinthians 4:4. Which Greek word did he use and what was its meaning at the time?

### Claude · 2025-12-31 15:36

**Greek word**: κύριος (*kyrios*)

**Meaning at the time**:

1. **General Greek usage**: "master," "owner," "one with authority over" — applied to slave owners, property holders, or as a respectful address ("sir").
2. **Septuagint (LXX) usage**: The standard Greek translation for both **YHWH** (the Tetragrammaton) and **Adonai** (אֲדֹנָי). This is the critical background for Paul's usage — when Greek-speaking Jews read their scriptures, *kyrios* was the word they encountered for God's name.
3. **Paul's application**: Paul applies *kyrios* to Jesus, thereby attributing to Christ the title the LXX reserved for YHWH. In 1 Corinthians 4:4 specifically ("ὁ δὲ ἀνακρίνων με κύριός ἐστιν"), Paul identifies Christ as his judge.
4. **Imperial context**: *Kyrios* was increasingly used as a title for the Roman Emperor, carrying connotations of divine authority. Paul's insistence on Christ as *kyrios* carried implicit political tension with imperial claims.

The term was semantically loaded — simultaneously denoting ownership/authority in everyday speech while carrying divine-name associations from Jewish scripture.

### Millard · 2025-12-31 15:58

When Jesus told Thomas to put his hand in the wound in his side and his finger in the wound in his hand and told him to believe, tell me about the Greek word for "believe". Pistis?

### Claude · 2025-12-31 15:58

*Pistis* (πίστις) is the noun ("faith," "belief," "trust").

In John 20:27, the words used are adjectives, not the noun:

**μὴ γίνου ἄπιστος ἀλλὰ πιστός**
("do not be unbelieving but believing")

* **ἄπιστος** (*apistos*) — unbelieving, faithless
* **πιστός** (*pistos*) — believing, faithful

Same root (πιστ-), but adjectival forms rather than the noun *pistis* or the verb *pisteuō* (πιστεύω, "I believe").

**Semantic range of the root at the time**:

* Trust, confidence, reliability
* Faithfulness, loyalty
* Persuasion, conviction

The word carried relational weight — not merely intellectual assent but confidence in/loyalty toward a person or claim. In commercial contexts, it denoted trustworthiness or a binding commitment.

### Millard · 2025-12-31 15:59

so "be persuaded"?

### Claude · 2025-12-31 15:59

Partially, but with a distinction.

**Etymological connection**: πίστις/πιστός are related to πείθω (*peithō*, "to persuade"). The perfect passive of πείθω (πέποιθα) means "I have been persuaded" → "I trust."

**However**: By the koine period, πιστός as an adjective describes a *state* or *character quality* — "trusting," "faithful," "reliable" — rather than the *process* of being persuaded.

In John 20:27, the construction γίνου πιστός ("become believing/faithful") emphasizes transitioning into a state of trust, not merely receiving persuasion.

**More precise rendering**: "Do not be/become a distrustful one, but a trusting one."

"Be persuaded" captures the receptive dimension but loses the characterological emphasis — Jesus is calling Thomas to *be a certain kind of person*, not just to accept a proposition.

### Millard · 2025-12-31 16:02

In English, don't "belief" and "believe" always refer to propositions?

### Claude · 2025-12-31 16:02

No.

"I believe in you" — this is relational (trust, confidence in a person), not propositional. It doesn't mean "I believe that you exist."

"I believe him" — I trust what he says, confidence in his reliability.

These are fiduciary uses — trust placed in a person rather than assent to a proposition.

**However**, the unmarked usage ("I believe X") does default to propositional content in English:

* "I believe he's coming" = I believe *that* he is coming
* "I believe it's true" = assent to a proposition

So English "believe" can carry relational meaning, but typically requires "in" or a personal object to activate it. The propositional sense is the default.

This is relevant to translation: rendering *pistos* as "believing" in English risks pulling the meaning toward propositional assent, when the Greek carries stronger relational/characterological weight. "Trusting" or "faithful" might preserve that dimension better.

How many times does the Greek word for "propitiation" appear in the NT?

### Claude · 2025-12-31 19:17

Two related Greek words are involved:

**hilasmos** (*hilasmos*): 2 occurrences

* 1 John 2:2
* 1 John 4:10

**hilastērion** (*hilastērion*): 2 occurrences

* Romans 3:25
* Hebrews 9:5 (here referring to the "mercy seat" — the ark's cover — not "propitiation" as a theological concept)

So: 4 total occurrences of the word group, 3 with theological "propitiation" sense, depending on how you classify Romans 3:25 (some argue *hilastērion* there also means "mercy seat" metaphorically rather than "propitiation").

The translation "propitiation" itself is contested — some render these as "expiation" (removing sin) rather than "propitiation" (appeasing wrath), which carries different theological weight.
