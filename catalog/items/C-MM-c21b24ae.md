Are brown widows and false widows also poisonous

### Claude · 2026-09-28 17:49

Yes, both are venomous, but both are much milder in practice than a black widow. A brown widow injects less venom, so bites usually cause only local pain and redness. A false widow bite is typically like a bee sting.

### Millard · 2026-09-28 23:08

Why did the opt for alternating current instead of direct current in the United States and then the rest of North America

### Claude · 2026-09-28 23:08

Because AC could be stepped up by transformers to high voltage for cheap long-distance transmission and stepped back down for homes, while 1880s DC couldn't, so Edison's DC system needed a power plant roughly every mile. Westinghouse's AC system, built on Tesla's patents, won the "War of the Currents" by the mid-1890s, sealed by the Niagara Falls hydro project in 1895. Canada and Mexico adopted the same AC standard, mostly because they bought American equipment and tied into U.S. grids.

### Millard · 2026-09-28 23:12

How did the Pinkerton agency get started

### Claude · 2026-09-28 23:13

Allan Pinkerton, a Scottish immigrant barrel-maker turned Chicago detective, founded it around 1850 as a private detective agency. Its early business was guarding railroads against train robbers and thieves.

### Millard · 2026-09-28 23:13

How did it get to be so large and formidable

### Claude · 2026-09-28 23:13

It filled the gap left by the lack of any national police force. Its fame grew when it foiled the 1861 Baltimore plot against Lincoln and ran Union intelligence in the Civil War. After the war it chased famous outlaws like the Reno Gang, Jesse James, and Butch Cassidy across state lines, which local sheriffs couldn't do, and it built the country's first large criminal records file of mugshots and descriptions. Its real muscle and money came from industrialists who hired it to spy on unions and break strikes, peaking with the armed battle at Homestead in 1892.

### Millard · 2026-09-28 23:14

What happened at homestead

### Claude · 2026-09-28 23:14

In 1892 Carnegie Steel, run by Henry Clay Frick, locked the union out of its Homestead mill near Pittsburgh after contract talks over pay cuts collapsed. On July 6, about 300 armed Pinkertons came up the river on barges to retake the plant. Strikers and townspeople met them, and a day-long gunfight left roughly 7 workers and 3 Pinkertons dead. The Pinkertons surrendered and were beaten as they were marched out through the crowd. The governor then sent in about 8,000 state militia, the mill reopened with replacement workers, and the union was crushed. That broke organized labor in American steel for about 40 years.
