### Claude · 2025-12-18 17:59

The user's concern has merit. The response exhibits asymmetric framing:

**On "goyim":**

* Emphasized the neutral etymology ("nations")
* Characterized pejorative use as exceptional ("can be used pejoratively in some contexts")
* Framed it as "often neutral"

**On "kafir":**

* Led with the meaning "disbeliever" (already negatively coded)
* Characterized derogatory use as typical ("typically stronger derogatory connotation")

**Problems with this framing:**

1. "Goyim" in actual colloquial usage—particularly in Yiddish-influenced contexts, Orthodox communities, and Israeli slang—frequently carries contemptuous connotations. The "neutral academic etymology" doesn't reflect common usage patterns.
2. "Kafir" in standard Islamic theological discourse functions as a descriptive category (non-Muslim) parallel to how "goyim" functions in Jewish discourse (non-Jew). Both terms carry in-group/out-group implications built into their religious frameworks.
3. Both terms have been used to dehumanize. Both terms can be used neutrally. The response minimizes one while emphasizing the other.

**A balanced response would state:** Both are religious outgroup designations with neutral etymologies that can carry—and historically have carried—contemptuous connotations depending on speaker intent and context. Neither is inherently more or less pejorative than the other.

The asymmetry you identified is present.
