### Claude · 2025-07-12 20:22

The contagion experiments conducted by the U.S. Navy and Public Health Service during the 1918-1919 influenza pandemic were legitimate scientific studies, not conspiratorial activities. These experiments were led by Dr. Milton Joseph Rosenau and conducted by the U.S. Public Health Service and the U.S. Navy, aimed at understanding how the deadly flu spread.

The experiments took place at two quarantine stations: Gallops Island in Boston Harbor and Angel Island in San Francisco. The researchers used 100 volunteers from the Navy who had no history of influenza to test various transmission methods.

The study methods were surprisingly direct but conducted with volunteer consent:

* Researchers sprayed live flu bacteria directly into their noses, throats, and eyes—yet none got sick
* Volunteers shook hands, spoke at close range, and even allowed infected patients to cough in their faces—still, no flu cases developed
* Blood transfusions from flu patients were directly injected into volunteers—no transmission occurred

The results were completely unexpected and baffling to the researchers. None of the volunteers in these experiments developed influenza, despite what seemed like certain exposure to the most contagious disease of the time.

Dr. Rosenau was honest about the puzzling results, stating: "We entered the outbreak with a notion that we knew the cause of the disease, and were quite sure we knew how it was transmitted from person to person. Perhaps, if we have learned anything, it is that we are not quite sure what we know about the disease".

These experiments weren't cover-ups or unethical research—they were desperate attempts by scientists to understand a pandemic that was killing millions. The failure to transmit the flu in controlled conditions highlighted the limitations of medical knowledge at the time, as researchers didn't yet understand that influenza was viral (not bacterial) and that transmission patterns were more complex than they assumed.

The studies represent an important chapter in the history of epidemiology and demonstrate how scientific understanding of infectious diseases has evolved significantly since 1918.
