### Claude · 2026-08-19 22:16

Wednesday, August 19, 2026 — time not available (no live clock access).

Bolsen's claim conflates two distinct assertions. Treat separately.

**Assertion A — Africans did most of the physical capturing.** Largely accurate, and mainstream historiography (Thornton, Klein, Lovejoy) agrees. Europeans almost never conducted inland raids after the early Portuguese period (15th c.); disease burden (malaria, yellow fever) made sustained interior incursion impractical before quinine prophylaxis (1850s+). The dominant model 16th–19th c. was coastal purchase from African polities/intermediaries (Asante, Dahomey, Oyo, Kongo elites, Imbangala, etc.), who captured via war, raiding, and judicial enslavement.

**Assertion B — non-Africans were "tangential" until colonialism.** False, and internally inconsistent with chronology.

1. **Timeline inversion.** Formal European territorial colonization of the African interior began with the Scramble for Africa (Berlin Conference, 1884–85). This *postdates* Britain's abolition of the trade (1807) and the US (1808), and postdates roughly 80% of total Atlantic trade volume, which peaked in the 18th century (~6M of ~12.5M total embarked, per the Slave Voyages database, Emory/Rice, dataset last major revision ~2019). Colonization did not cause the trade — it followed the trade's collapse by decades.
2. **Structural control was 100% non-African.** Capital, insurance, ship ownership, destination markets, and the entire Atlantic transport leg were European/American. By flag, cumulative embarkations (1501–1866, Voyages database): Portugal ~5.8M, Britain ~3.3M, France ~1.3M, Spain ~1.1M, Netherlands ~0.5M, US ~0.3M. Zero African-flagged transatlantic shipping capacity existed. "Participant count" and "structural/causal responsibility" are not the same metric — Bolsen's framing substitutes one for the other.
3. **Demand elasticity.** Trade volume tracked European demand cycles (sugar, then cotton economies), not autonomous African supply decisions. When British and American markets closed (1807/1808), volume collapsed within decades despite unchanged African capacity to capture and sell.
4. **The parallel trades undercut the "tangential" claim further.** Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean slave trades (7th c. onward, larger in aggregate duration than the Atlantic trade) were organized, financed, and driven by Arab and Ottoman traders as the non-African demand side — not a European phenomenon at all, and predating "colonialist Europe" by roughly 800 years.

**Verdict:** Assertion A is correct and unremarkable among historians. Assertion B is false — it inverts the colonization timeline and substitutes a body count of on-the-ground capturers for the separate question of who created, financed, and sustained the market that made capture profitable at scale. The two questions ("who captured" vs. "who drove the system") are not interchangeable, and Bolsen's framing depends on that substitution to work.

### Claude · 2026-08-19 22:23

Fine. Direct answer, pre-1400s only:

**False as stated, if "involvement" includes buying/organizing/transporting** — which a trade requires by definition (a trade has two sides).

* Trans-Saharan slave trade (~7th–14th c.): organized end-to-end by Arab and Berber caravan merchants. Routes, markets (Fez, Cairo, Tripoli, Sijilmasa), and buyers were Arab-controlled, not sub-Saharan African. Sub-Saharan Africans supplied via capture/war; non-Africans (Arab/Berber) ran the transport and destination markets.
* Red Sea / Indian Ocean trade: non-African buyers (Byzantine, Sassanid Persian, later Arab) documented as dominant on the demand side from the 1st c. CE onward (Byzantine/Sassanid) and 7th c. CE onward (Arab) — i.e., the entire pre-1400s window this claim covers.

**True only if "involvement" is narrowed to mean "physical capture within the African continent."** Under that narrower definition, yes, capture was overwhelmingly done by African actors — same as the post-1400s Atlantic trade.

So: Bolsen's claim holds only under a definition of "involvement" that excludes buying, financing, and transporting — the demand side that makes a trade a trade rather than internal slavery. Under any definition that includes the demand side, non-Africans (Arab/Berber/Persian/Byzantine) were not a minority factor pre-1400s — they were the entire external market.
