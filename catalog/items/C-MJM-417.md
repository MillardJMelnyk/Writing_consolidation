### Claude · 2025-12-07 17:03

Several structural problems with this hypothesis:

**Empirical gaps:**

1. The claim that Homo "realized" domination was destructive and diverged from it lacks a mechanism. How does a species-level realization occur? How does it become heritable? Paleoanthropological evidence of interpersonal violence extends well before 10-15K years (Jebel Sahaba, ~13K BP; evidence of trauma in much older specimens).
2. The marine mammal aside is factually wrong. Elephant seals engage in brutal dominance contests. Dolphin males form coalitions to coerce females. Orcas have hierarchical pod structures. Dominance behavior is widespread in cetaceans and pinnipeds.

**Logical problems:**

3. You're positing a "healthy baseline" human without domination impulses, then explaining dominators as aberrant specimens. This inverts the burden of proof. What's your evidence for the baseline rather than assuming it?
4. The 10-15K timeline corresponds to agricultural surplus enabling stratification—a material explanation that doesn't require a "psychotic minority" hypothesis.
5. "Evil impulse" moralizes the phenomenon before analyzing it. If domination has psychosocial roots (as your own work on codependence suggests), calling it "evil" may obscure rather than clarify the mechanism.

**The actual question:**

Are you proposing this as a historical hypothesis to be tested, or as a rhetorical frame for justifying a present project (ending domination cycles)? The latter doesn't require the former to be true.

### Claude · 2025-12-07 17:12

**Coherence:** No problem. The components fit together:

* Species-level shifts occur (language, theory of mind, extended childhood)
* Such shifts don't require 100% uniformity—variation persists
* A cooperative majority being vulnerable to an organized domination-oriented minority is internally consistent, not contradictory

**Already demonstrated unlikely:** No.

Christopher Boehm's work on egalitarian hunter-gatherers actually points in a compatible direction—he documents active suppression of dominance attempts ("reverse dominance hierarchy") across diverse forager societies. This suggests dominance-orientation is something humans learned to *counteract*, which is at least adjacent to your hypothesis.

The standard "humans are naturally hierarchical" narrative has its own evidentiary problems—it leans heavily on chimpanzee analogies while downplaying bonobos and the actual diversity of human social organization.

The 10-15K BP transition evidence (surplus-enabled stratification) is typically read as *revealing* latent human hierarchy. Your reading—that it enabled pathological outliers to exploit a cooperative baseline—is non-mainstream but not refuted. It's an interpretive difference about the same data.

**Summary:** The hypothesis is coherent and open. It's not established, but neither is it foreclosed by existing evidence. The question is what would differentiate it from alternatives empirically.
