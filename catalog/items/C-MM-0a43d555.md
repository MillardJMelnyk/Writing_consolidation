### Claude · 2026-10-27 17:58

**Verdict:** As a newcomer reading only this report, and putting the no-link evidence under extra challenge, I'd call a link between the combined early schedule and autism **Unlikely, about 25%**, and a link confined to a vulnerable subgroup **Unlikely, about 40%**. The report's headline claim, that vaccination is "the most significant modifiable risk factor," isn't supported by its own evidence.

**Evidence for a link**

1. Table 4 holds 11 datasets, not 12, because Garner 2020 and Garner 2022 analyze the same 1,482 people. Only five give an internal vaccinated-versus-unvaccinated autism comparison: Mawson 2017, Hooker & Miller 2021, NVKP, Mawson 2025, and Henry Ford. The rest report no autism figure, compare against national averages, or compare early versus later-or-never hepatitis B. All 11 point the same way.
2. The two strongest are Mawson 2025 and Henry Ford, and they move me more than anything else in the report.  
   - Mawson 2025: 47,155 Medicaid kids, autism 2.81% vaccinated versus 1.05% unvaccinated. It climbs with vaccination visits: 9, 15, 24, and 40 per 1,000.  
   - Henry Ford: 18,468 kids, neurodevelopmental disorders 5.5 times higher in the vaccinated (2.9–10.5). Autism alone was 23 cases versus 1, which by itself could happen by chance 25% of the time.
3. The surveys (Mawson 2017, Hooker & Miller 2021, NVKP, Garner) recruited through vaccine-critical groups, homeschool networks, or single practices, and used parent-reported diagnoses. I give each about a quarter the weight of a records-based study.
4. Some effect sizes are extreme: Hooker & Miller 2021 found ADHD at 20.8 times and ear infections at 27.8 times. I take the direction at face value, but sizes like that need replication before I'd count them.
5. Timing signals carry extra weight because two of them come from the no-link side's own data:  
   - Verstraeten's internal CDC analysis found autism 7.6 times higher (1.8–31.5) with more than 25 µg of mercury in the first month.  
   - DeStefano 2004 found vaccination before 36 months more common in autism cases (OR 1.49).  
   - Gallagher 2010 found autism 3 times higher in boys given hepatitis B as newborns.
6. The mechanistic work is plausible, but the doses are high. For example, the rat glutamate study used 240 µg of mercury per kg, versus about 5 µg/kg per thimerosal shot in a 5 kg infant, roughly 50 times higher. Low weight.
7. The "107 versus 29" tally isn't evidence. Of the roughly 88 entries in S3, only about 14 are human studies testing vaccine exposure against autism or neurodevelopmental outcomes, and most of those are ecological or adverse-event-report analyses. The rest are:  
   - animal and cell studies  
   - industrial-mercury studies  
   - febrile-seizure studies  
   - narrative reviews  
   - one interview study of what parents believe

S2 holds about 19 human studies of that kind.

**Evidence against a link, under extra challenge**

8. Holt found 55% of Danish registry "MMR-unvaccinated" children were actually vaccinated. At that rate a true doubling of risk would show up as about 1.3, so the Danish MMR studies can't rule out a modest effect. I discount them heavily.
9. Verstraeten's 7.6 becoming "no consistent association" in print, and Thompson's statement on DeStefano 2004, are documented softening of positive signals. I cut the weight of the CDC-authored studies by half.
10. The Danish aluminum study of 1.2 million kids survives the challenge. Even the critics' own reanalysis finds an excess of only 8.68 per 10,000 across dose levels, which is 0.09 percentage points, against the 1.76-point gap in Mawson 2025. If aluminum plays a role, it's small.
11. Mrozek-Budzyn 2010 also survives:
    * It's an independent Polish study with specialist-confirmed autism and vaccination taken from medical records and parent cards.
    * It found MMR versus unvaccinated at OR 0.17 and any vaccine versus none at OR 0.28.
    * It meets every standard the report demands, compares against unvaccinated kids, and points the other way. It isn't in Table 4, and its effect size gets the same replication caution as item 4.
12. In Yokohama, autism rose from 48–86 to 97–161 per 10,000 after MMR was withdrawn. That rules out MMR alone as the driver, not the full schedule.

**The report's own reliability**

13. The report says none of the neutral studies did independent clinical assessments, yet its own S2 lists five that did: Price, DeStefano 2013, Baird, Hornig, and Mrozek-Budzyn.
14. There are internal errors:
    * Garner is counted twice.
    * The Henry Ford row gives rates of 0.0011 and 0.0009 per 1,000, contradicting the report's own text.
    * Holt, which is a vaccination-coverage audit, is filed as a "neutral" autism study.
    * The retracted Lyons-Weiler paper is cited without noting the retraction.
15. The report declares "nothing to declare" on conflicts while listing Wakefield Media Group as an affiliation and using a figure from Children's Health Defense. That earns the same discount I gave the CDC-authored work.

**Bottom line**

* Combined schedule raises autism risk population-wide: **Unlikely, ~25%**
* Raises risk in a vulnerable subgroup (preterm, early newborn dosing): **Unlikely, ~40%**
* Vaccination is the largest modifiable risk factor, as the report claims: **Unlikely, ~15%**

The entire gap between this and my earlier 6–7% comes from the outside evidence this exercise set aside.
