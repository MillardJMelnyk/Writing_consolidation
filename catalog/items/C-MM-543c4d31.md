### Millard · 2026-05-25 21:12

What's the difference between organic arsenic and toxic arsenic

### Claude · 2026-05-25 21:12

The key distinction is chemical form, not just the element itself.

**Inorganic arsenic** (the toxic kind) — arsenic bonded to non-carbon elements (oxygen, sulfur, chlorine). Found in groundwater, soil, pesticides, and industrial processes. Forms like arsenite (As³⁺) and arsenate (As⁵⁺) are highly toxic, carcinogenic, and bioaccumulate. This is what causes arsenic poisoning.

**Organic arsenic** — arsenic bonded to carbon. Found naturally in seafood (fish, shellfish, seaweed). Common forms are arsenobetaine and arsenocholine. These pass through the body largely unmetabolized and are considered essentially non-toxic at normal dietary exposure levels.

The liver can also convert inorganic arsenic into methylated organic forms (monomethylarsonic acid, dimethylarsinic acid) as a detox pathway — though those methylated forms aren't completely harmless either, just less acutely toxic.

**Practical upshot:** eating shrimp gives you organic arsenic, which is fine. Drinking contaminated well water gives you inorganic arsenic, which is not.

So why are we told to limit our intake of rice due to its arsenic content

### Claude · 2026-05-25 21:13

Because the arsenic in rice is predominantly **inorganic** arsenic — the toxic kind.

Rice is uniquely efficient at absorbing inorganic arsenic from soil and water for two reasons:

1. **Flooded paddies** create anaerobic (low-oxygen) conditions that convert soil-bound arsenic into soluble forms, making it highly bioavailable in the water the rice roots sit in.
2. **Rice physiology** — it takes up silicon, and arsenic mimics silicon chemically, so rice absorbs it readily through the same transport channels.

Brown rice is worse than white because the bran layer (where arsenic concentrates) is retained. Rice bran and rice syrup used as food ingredients are also high.

The concern is especially for:  
- Infants eating rice cereal as a first food  
- People eating rice as a staple multiple times daily  
- Populations in South/Southeast Asia where both rice consumption and groundwater arsenic levels are high

The risk isn't from occasional rice eating — it's cumulative chronic low-dose inorganic arsenic exposure linked to bladder, lung, and skin cancers over decades.

Rinsing rice and cooking it in excess water (then draining) removes a meaningful portion of the arsenic, though not all.
