### Claude · 2025-12-03 16:40

The specific claim about skeletal remains in tunnels between monasteries and convents is largely folklore. A medievalist notes that tales about "dead babies found behind convents" are "an old, old ghost story" that "is pretty common all over Europe, and especially in England, where it gained a lot of strength following the dissolution of the monasteries by Henry VIII." It appears to be "just one of those ugly things that people say."

However, your broader point stands: Yes, Christians did practice infanticide motivated by shame—functionally similar to honor killings, though not named as such.

**The documented reality:**

In Christian countries, infanticide had two main drivers: "the disgrace attendant upon illegitimacy" and economic reasons. Illegitimate children were "sacrificed partly for the concealment of shame." This directly parallels honor killing motivation.

When the Roman Empire collapsed in the 5th century, the Church initially treated infanticide as sin rather than crime, with punishment of public penance for 7-15 years. But the Council of Florence decreed in 1439 that unbaptized infants descend to hell—turning infanticide into "the most heinous of all crimes." Despite harsher penalties, it continued. "Churches and society continued to vilify illegitimate birth, thus enhancing rather than preventing infanticide."

**Specific to celibacy violation:** Medieval records document pregnant nuns being hidden in convents to give birth in secret, with children sometimes killed or abandoned afterward. While "it was not considered a terrible dishonor to be illegitimate in that age," the institutional pressure on convents to avoid scandal created conditions for infanticide.

The functional mechanism is identical: shame-driven killing to preserve institutional and family honor. The Christian church didn't systematize it as doctrine, but tolerated and enabled it through suppression of unmarried sexuality while providing no safe alternatives.

### Claude · 2025-12-03 16:42

There isn't a single recognized cultural name equivalent to "honor killing."

It was simply called "infanticide" or "child murder" — clinical terms that obscured the shame-driven motivation. Historians studying specific cases refer to it as "concealment infanticide" when the context was hiding illegitimacy, but this never gained currency as a cultural category.

That's the distinction: Islamic cultures openly named and discussed honor killing as a practice (even if condemned); Christian societies treated infanticide as individual criminal acts or sins, never as a culturally-sanctioned practice deserving its own category. The institutional suppression of discussion — calling it merely "murder" rather than "shame murder" — made it invisible as a system.

If you needed a parallel name for rhetorical purposes, "concealment infanticide" or "shame infanticide" would be accurate, but neither has historical recognition.
