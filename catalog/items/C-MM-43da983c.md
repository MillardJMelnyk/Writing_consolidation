### Claude · 2026-09-06 15:03

I'll check the falsifiable claims rather than answer from recall.

**Verdict: the two load-bearing factual claims are backwards in time, and the "no proof of a virus" claim misstates the published record. Some of the surrounding history is accurate.**

**What holds up**

Lead arsenate was real and heavily used. It was first prepared in 1892 in Massachusetts against the gypsy moth, quickly adopted in fruit orchards, and was the most widely used pesticide in the nation — recommended by USDA and applied to millions of acres — until the late 1940s. It was applied with hand sprayers that drenched the trees, and residue on fruit caused public alarm as early as 1919. Polio outbreaks were summer-seasonal. "Poliomyelitis" is a pathological term for inflammation of the spinal cord gray matter, not the name of an organism — that part is correct. The book is Forrest **Maready**, *The Moth in the Iron Lung* (he garbles the author and title).

**Claim 1 — polio "went away right after these toxic pesticides stopped being used." Backwards.**

Lead arsenate use was discontinued following the introduction of DDT in 1948 and organophosphates in the early 1950s; phased out on most Washington apple crops by 1947. US polio then hit its **all-time peak in 1952** — more than 21,000 paralytic cases (the widely quoted 57,879 is *total* reported polio, paralytic plus non-paralytic; the two numbers get conflated constantly). Lead arsenate collapsed first, polio peaked after.

**Claim 2 — DDT "taken out of widespread use... a couple of years before the vaccine." Flatly false.**

US DDT use peaked at approximately 80 million pounds in 1959 and only then declined, reaching just under 12 million pounds by the early 1970s; US production peaked in the early 1960s at 82,000 tonnes in 1962. The vaccine was 1955. So DDT usage was still *climbing* to its maximum during exactly the years polio was collapsing: from more than 21,000 paralytic cases in 1952 to 2,525 in 1960 and 61 in 1965. Both his correlations run the wrong direction at the decisive moment. This is not interpretation; it is two curves.

**Claim 3 — "never been any proof that any virus causes this."**

The experiments exist and are published. Landsteiner and Popper took spinal cord from a nine-year-old who died in three days; bacterial cultures of the cord were sterile; injection into rabbits, guinea pigs and mice gave nothing; the same material injected into two monkeys produced extensive spinal cord lesions closely resembling human poliomyelitis in one, and complete flaccid paralysis of both legs in the other. Preparations were passed through filters known to trap bacteria, and the filtrates still produced polio in monkeys. The disease was then passed monkey to monkey. You can argue about what that proves. You cannot say no such evidence was produced. Recalled, not verified here: Enders, Weller and Robbins grew the agent in non-neural tissue in 1949; poliovirus was later crystallized and, in 1981, cloned DNA alone was shown to generate infectious virus.

**Claim 4 — his own premise cuts against him.** He says polio has a characteristic microscopic lesion. It does: destruction of anterior horn motor neurons, asymmetric, pure motor. Arsenic doesn't produce that. Arsenic polyneuropathy is an axonal-loss disease of peripheral nerve with low or absent *sensory* and motor responses and distal denervation, with sensory complaints more common than motor, and it is characteristically mistaken for Guillain-Barré, not for polio. Different tissue, different lesion, different clinical picture.

**Claim 5 — the timing of the epidemics doesn't fit the spraying.** Outbreaks near Oslo in 1868 and northern Sweden in 1881 preceded the 132-case Vermont outbreak of 1894; Sweden had 1,031 cases in 1905 and 3,840 in 1911, during which Kling's group recovered the agent from healthy carriers as well as paralytic patients. Scandinavian epidemics on that scale aren't explained by New England gypsy moth spraying.

**The evidence he never addresses: the 1954 trial.**

623,972 schoolchildren were injected with vaccine or placebo and over a million more served as observed controls; the result was 80–90% effectiveness against paralytic polio. Of the fifteen children who died with polio in the trial, all fifteen were in the placebo or observed control groups. Same towns, same summer, same orchards, same DDT. Two arms differing only in what was in the syringe. No pesticide-exposure model produces a difference between those arms. Honest caveat, since it's a real criticism of that trial: the placebo was culture medium ("Medium 199"), not saline — which matters for reading the *safety* arm, not the efficacy comparison.

**Things worth conceding on his side**

Provocation paralysis is documented — injections during an outbreak raised paralytic risk in the injected limb, so non-viral cofactors are real. The 1955 Cutter batches caused paralytic cases. Today more paralytic polio worldwide is vaccine-derived than wild — the 2022 New York case was vaccine-derived type 2. And I recall, without having checked it here, that reporting criteria for "paralytic polio" tightened around 1955, which inflates the apparent post-vaccine drop by some disputed amount. The genuine open question in this history is *why* polio turned epidemic in the cleanest, richest countries around 1880–1950; the delayed-exposure explanation is inference, not measurement.

**The last third of the clip**

"Herd exposure to a toxin → pandemic of internal reactions," homeopathic miasms, "entire races of people... holographically," and German New Medicine described as "evidence-based" — none of that is argument. GNM rests on Hamer's own case interpretations; I know of no controlled test of it, and calling it evidence-based is an assertion with nothing behind it. That section is vocabulary, not a claim you could check.

**Bottom line:** the pesticide hypothesis is a legitimate thing to have asked in 1950. Checked against dates, it fails — twice, in the same direction — and Kaufman states the failures as if they were the confirmations.

### Millard · 2026-09-06 15:28

How did they determine that the ostensible polio virus had adequately been "attenuated"?

### Claude · 2026-09-06 15:28

By injecting it directly into monkey spinal cords and counting the damage.

Cynomolgus monkeys are inoculated intraspinally, killed about 17 days later, and the cord and brain sectioned — lesions scored at 7 sites in the brain, 12 in the cervical cord, and 18 in the lumbar cord, left and right, averaged into a "lesion score." Low score plus low paralysis incidence in a comparison against a virulent reference strain is the criterion. That's still the WHO standard test; a transgenic-mouse version measuring the dose that paralyzes 50% of animals is now used alongside or in place of it.

So "attenuated" was and is defined operationally — less damage than the reference strain under the same assay — not by any independent measure of safety in people.
