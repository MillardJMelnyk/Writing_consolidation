### Millard · 2026-08-03 17:15

The way English translations of the New Testament use the word "works" has always bothered me. BRIEFLY explain what the original words actually meant.

### Claude · 2026-08-03 17:15

The Greek is **ἔργα** (*erga*), plural of *ergon* — simply "things done," deeds, actions. Concrete, not abstract. Where English says "works," Greek says "the stuff you actually did."

That's the whole problem. English "works" sounds like a category — a theological currency you accumulate. Greek *erga* is just the plural of "a thing you did." "Faith without deeds is dead" reads very differently from "faith without works is dead," and the second is the mistranslation of tone, not vocabulary.

Paul's key phrase is **ἔργα νόμου** (*erga nomou*), "deeds of law" — actions the Torah prescribes. He's not contrasting faith with doing good things generally; he's contrasting trust with law-observance as the basis of standing before God. When translators drop "of law" or let plain "works" carry the freight, Paul appears to be against effort itself. He isn't.

James uses *erga* in the ordinary sense — conduct, what you do — which is why he and Paul look like they're fighting when they're using the same word for different things.

Two other words also collapse into English "work": **κόπος** (*kopos*), toil to the point of exhaustion, and **πρᾶξις** (*praxis*), habitual practice.

### Claude · 2026-08-03 17:16

Correct — I was sloppy. ἔργα never means "prescriptions." *Erga* are performed things, always; the noun itself carries the doing. Requirements would be ἐντολαί, or Paul's own δικαίωμα τοῦ νόμου, "the law's requirement" (Rom 8:4) — a different word precisely because it's a different thing.

So νόμου isn't "prescribed by." It's the sphere or character the deeds belong to: deeds done in the law-mode, observances actually racked up by someone operating under law. Not a checklist. A performance record.

That's the whole argument in Rom 3:20 — no one is justified by his tally of law-observances. Paul isn't evaluating the law's contents; he's evaluating what people did and what they claim it earned them.

The Qumran parallel backs it: מעשי התורה (*ma'ase ha-torah*) in 4QMMT means the group's actual observances, the practices marking who's inside. Performed, not prescribed.

### Claude · 2026-08-03 17:17

"Rationalized" overreaches — it smuggles in a motive Paul isn't asserting in the phrase itself. "Authorized" is close but tilts toward permission.

The genitive is looser than either. It marks belonging, not licensing: deeds that are law-deeds, done as law-observance, by someone living inside that system. The person doing them isn't justifying himself with a rationale — he's just observing Torah, which is what he'd do regardless.

Paul's charge comes separately, in the sentences around the phrase, not inside it. Rom 3:27 — boasting. Rom 10:3 — establishing their own righteousness. That's where the "rationalized" part lives, and Paul has to argue for it, precisely because the phrase alone doesn't say it.

So: deeds done as law-observance. What Paul thinks people wrongly conclude from having done them is his argument, not the phrase's meaning.

### Claude · 2026-08-03 17:27

Different sense entirely — James never says *erga nomou*, just *erga*. Bare deeds. Whatever you actually did.

His examples make it plain: feed the naked and hungry (2:15-16), Abraham offering Isaac, Rahab hiding the spies. None of those are Torah observances. Rahab isn't even Jewish. He's not talking about the law-mode at all — he's talking about whether anything happened.

So the argument isn't faith-versus-works. It's about whether the noun has a referent. James 2:14 asks whether *that kind* of faith — the kind with no deeds attached — can save. His answer is that it isn't a defective faith, it's a word with nothing behind it. Dead: νεκρά, a corpse. Same shape as saying you have a car and never having driven it.

"Deeds" or "actions" translates it cleanly. And the Paul conflict mostly evaporates: Paul denies that law-observance establishes standing; James denies that claimed belief without conduct is belief. They're not answering the same question. Where they do collide is Abraham — both cite Gen 15:6, and James says Abraham was justified by deeds, which is verbally Paul's opposite. But James is reading the Isaac episode as the demonstration of the trust Genesis 15 already credited; Paul is reading the credit itself as prior to circumcision. Different moments, same story.

### Millard · 2026-08-03 17:27

### Claude · 2026-08-03 17:27

Yes. Same word, same meaning: performed deeds. The difference is entirely in what's attached — νόμου in Paul, nothing in James.

That's worth sitting with, because it dismantles the traditional framing. "Works" was never a theological category that Paul rejected and James defended. It's an ordinary noun. Neither of them is arguing about *erga*; they're arguing about different things that happen to involve deeds.

And Paul uses bare *erga* positively without flinching — ἔργον ἀγαθόν, good deeds, all over the place. Eph 2:10: created for good deeds. Two verses after "not of deeds, lest anyone boast." He doesn't notice a tension because there isn't one in the Greek. The tension is manufactured downstream, when "works" hardens into a single English abstraction and both men appear to be talking about it.
