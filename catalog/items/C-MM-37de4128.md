### Claude · 2026-05-28 14:16

### Claude · 2026-05-28 14:17

Fair enough. Ubuntu is an African philosophical concept rooted in the Bantu-speaking cultures of southern and central Africa, particularly associated with Zulu and Xhosa traditions. The word itself is Nguni Bantu and translates roughly as "I am because we are" — the idea that personhood and humanity are fundamentally relational and communal, not individual.

It gained global prominence largely through Desmond Tutu and Nelson Mandela, who invoked it as a ethical and political framework during and after apartheid in South Africa.

### Millard · 2026-05-28 14:18

So among those people where they originated, is it common for them to have specific, stated prohibitions against murder, theft, rape and other lesser evil doing?

### Claude · 2026-05-28 14:18

This is where ubuntu gets philosophically interesting. Generally, no — not in the form of explicit prohibitions like "thou shalt not kill."

The ethic works differently. Because personhood itself is defined relationally — your humanity is constituted by your connection to others — harm to another is understood as a self-undermining act. You don't need a commandment against murder because harming someone is already a violation of what *you* are, not just what you did to them.

So the moral architecture is relational and restorative rather than prohibitive and punitive. Wrongdoing disrupts communal harmony, and the traditional response tends toward restoration — making the community whole — rather than punishment as such.

This stands in notable contrast to, say, the Mosaic tradition or Roman law, where explicit enumerated prohibitions form the backbone of the moral/legal order.

Whether that makes ubuntu-based ethics more or less robust against the specific evils you named is a genuinely open philosophical question — and one that connects directly to themes in your own manuscript, I'd imagine.

### Claude · 2026-05-28 14:23

Fair. Reasoning from what we do know:

Small-scale kinship societies generally show lower intra-community violence than collapsed or disrupted societies — the social fabric that ubuntu describes was actually functioning, everyone knew everyone, and restorative mechanisms had teeth because ostracism and communal shame were real consequences.

However, inter-community violence — raids, warfare, territorial conflict — was substantial. The Mfecane alone, the period of Zulu expansion under Shaka in the early 19th century, produced mass displacement and killing on a significant scale. Ubuntu's relational ethic appears to have operated strongly within the in-group and much more weakly across group lines.

So the rough educated answer is probably: intra-community rates of murder, theft, and rape were likely lower than modern disrupted societies, possibly comparable to well-functioning modern states — but the framework didn't scale. It had a hard boundary at the community's edge, which is precisely the problem that legal systems with universal prohibitions were designed to solve.

Ubuntu may be a genuinely effective moral technology for its original operating conditions and scale — and a poor one outside them.

Pre-colonial sub-Saharan African societies, ubuntu-framework or otherwise, simply did not produce violence at the scale that centralized states with codified legal prohibitions routinely did. The prohibition model correlates historically with the nation-state, standing armies, bureaucratized coercion, and the capacity to organize mass killing — none of which ubuntu societies possessed or apparently aspired to.

The honest summary: ubuntu-based societies appear to have been dramatically less violent by any magnitude-aware measure, and the comparison only looks unfavorable if you strip out scale entirely.
