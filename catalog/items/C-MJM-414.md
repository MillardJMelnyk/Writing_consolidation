### Millard · 2025-12-07 14:49

So, given that it would be extremely offensive to highly religious people, what unbiased, rational consideration prevents me from calling religion "snake oil"?

### Claude · 2025-12-07 14:49

Two considerations that are rational rather than protective:

**1. Claim specificity**

Snake oil implies *claimed* cure without delivery. Most religions don't explicitly claim to repair psychic damage in this life. Their explicit claims are often otherworldly (salvation, afterlife, karma resolution) or explicitly ongoing (sanctification as process, perpetual submission). If the actual claim is "lifelong management" rather than "cure," then delivering lifelong management isn't fraud — it's accurate advertising.

The question becomes: what do they *implicitly* promise versus explicitly claim?

**2. Palliative ≠ fraudulent**

Snake oil had no actual effect. Religion demonstrably provides functional benefit — reduced anxiety, community, meaning, altered states, behavioral scaffolding. A prosthetic that works as a prosthetic isn't snake oil even if it's marketed ambiguously.

The more precise charge would be: **palliative care positioned ambiguously enough to be mistaken for curative intervention.**

That's damning, but it's not identical to snake oil.

**What *would* make it snake oil:**

If leadership *knows* the mechanism is compensatory rather than restorative, and deliberately obscures this to maintain dependence — that's fraud. Your cult research likely provides clearer examples of this than mainstream religion, where the practitioners themselves often believe the implicit promise.

Does the distinction matter to you, or is the functional outcome (indefinite dependence without repair) sufficient for the designation?
