**Common natural diuretics:**

* Caffeine (coffee, black/green tea) — mild to moderate
* Dandelion leaf (Taraxacum officinale) — one of the more evidence-backed
* Hibiscus tea
* Parsley
* Asparagus
* Celery seed
* Juniper berry
* Nettle leaf
* Alcohol — diuretic, but net hydration effect is negative

**Relevant to your situation:** if you're consuming any of these (especially caffeine or alcohol) in the afternoon/evening, that's a more likely driver of nocturia than total water volume — diuretics increase urine output disproportionate to fluid intake. Reducing volume without addressing diuretic intake often doesn't fix the problem.

If you're not consuming any of the above and nocturia persists after intake control, that's outside the scope of "try a supplement" — worth ruling out causes like BPH, sleep apnea, or blood sugar issues with a doctor rather than adding more diuretics.

### Millard · 2026-09-25 17:25

the point is to induce peeing earlier in the day so that at night it's already evacuated.

### Claude · 2026-09-25 17:25

That's a valid mechanism — front-loading fluid excretion via a morning dose so less remains to process overnight.

**Dandelion leaf extract** is the best-supported option for this: a small clinical study found measurable diuretic effect within 5 hours of dosing, tapering off after. Take it in the morning; effect should be substantially resolved by evening.

**Practical constraints:**

* It doesn't reduce total urine produced over 24h — it shifts timing. If your kidneys are processing a fixed fluid load, forcing more of it out early doesn't guarantee less is made later; renal fluid handling responds to hydration status continuously, not just to what you took that morning.
* Effect size is modest, not comparable to a prescription loop diuretic (furosemide) that shows a much stronger, more predictable acute effect.
* No controlled trials on dandelion extract specifically for nocturia reduction — this is inference from its diuretic pharmacokinetics, not a validated outcome study.

If dandelion doesn't measurably reduce night wakings after a trial period, the timing-shift strategy likely isn't addressing your actual cause (could be reduced bladder capacity at night, ADH dysregulation with age, or other causes independent of total fluid load) — worth medical evaluation rather than escalating dose.

### Millard · 2026-09-25 17:26

what's ADH dysregulation

### Claude · 2026-09-25 17:26

ADH = antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin), secreted by the posterior pituitary. Normal function: ADH rises at night, signaling kidneys to concentrate urine and reduce output during sleep — this is why healthy people don't need to void overnight.

**Dysregulation** = this nocturnal rise is blunted or absent. Kidneys keep producing dilute urine at the daytime rate through the night, so bladder fills faster than it should during sleep → wakes you to void.

**Causes:**

* Age-related decline in nocturnal ADH secretion (common after ~60)
* Diabetes insipidus (ADH deficiency or renal resistance to it) — distinct pathology, usually with much larger urine volumes
* Some medications (lithium, demeclocycline) impair renal response to ADH
* Sleep apnea — disrupts normal nocturnal hormone cycling, including ADH

**How it's distinguished from your fluid-timing issue:** if nocturia persists despite controlled evening intake and no evening diuretics, and urine at night is dilute (not concentrated like it should be), that points to ADH dysregulation rather than intake volume. A doctor can check this with a urine osmolality test — cheap, direct, not something you can self-assess.
