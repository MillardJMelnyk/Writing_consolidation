### Claude · 2026-06-20 15:35

Same idea, different status. "An attempt to achieve the good by force is like cutting out a man's eyes to give him a picture gallery" is the actual sentence Rand wrote — verifiable, attributable, quotable.

"You can't achieve anyone's good by force" is a paraphrase someone wrote and stuck in front of it. It's a fair restatement of the same claim, but it's not a Rand sentence — there's no citation for it as a quote. If you used it as a direct quote, you'd be quoting the poster-maker, not Rand.

So: same proposition, but only one of the two sentences in that image is actually hers.

No. She advocated a limited government — police, courts, military — with a monopoly on the legitimate use of force in a given territory. She explicitly argued against anarchism and against "competing governments" as incoherent.

### Millard · 2026-06-20 15:37

OK, to the extent that she retained the state, government, and authority, she subscribed to achieving good by force, right?

### Claude · 2026-06-20 15:38

Yes. Government, by her own account, is an institution that holds a monopoly on retaliatory force. So to the extent she kept the state, she kept an apparatus of force — and that apparatus is justified, in her framework, by the good it produces (rights protection, law, order). That's "achieving good by force" in plain terms, even though she'd object to the phrasing.

### Millard · 2026-06-20 15:44

So, "An attempt to achieve the good by force is like an attempt to provide a man with a picture gallery at the price of cutting out his eyes," UNLESS IT'S THE STATE THAT DOES IT.

### Claude · 2026-06-20 15:44

Right. Her actual carve-out: force is only legitimate as *retaliation* against someone who already initiated force or fraud — not as a means to produce a positive good (welfare, charity, redistribution, etc.) for someone. So her position is closer to:

"Force to produce someone's good is destructive — except retaliatory force used to stop/punish someone who violated rights, which she calls protective, not 'achieving good.'"

But functionally, yes — the state is the one institution she exempts from the force-destroys-the-beneficiary logic. Everyone else is bound by it; government isn't.
