### Millard · 2026-10-17 00:52

I have someone claiming "free market"="capitalism". There's nothing necessarily capitalistic about a free market, right?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 00:53

Friday, October 16, 2026, [time unavailable] CST

Right: a free market is a way of exchanging (voluntary trade, prices set by supply and demand), while capitalism is a way of owning (private owners of the means of production hiring wage labor), so you can have free markets without capitalism (Proudhon's mutualism, market socialism, worker co-op economies) and capitalism without free markets (crony and state capitalism).

### Millard · 2026-10-17 00:53

give me the major kinds of markets (capitalist, mutualist, ..."

### Claude · 2026-10-17 00:53

By ownership model, the major kinds are capitalist (private owners, wage labor), mutualist (individual/small-group possession, labor-cost exchange, mutual credit), market socialist (worker- or socially-owned firms competing in markets), distributist (widely dispersed small property, guilds), Georgist (private capital but land rents socialized), state capitalist (state-owned firms trading for profit), and mixed (private plus state sectors under regulation).

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:14

If you strip away all nonessential properties, what is ownership fundamentally, essentially about?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:14

Ownership is essentially a socially enforced right to exclude: you decide who may use a thing, and others back that decision.

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:15

Make up your mind. Decide who to exclude or who may use?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:15

Exclude: the power to say "no" is the core, and deciding who may use it is just that power exercised selectively.

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:16

Wouldn't it be more accurate to say it's a right to universally deprive?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:16

Mostly yes: legally, ownership is a right "in rem," held against the whole world except the owner, and calling that exclusion "deprivation" is accurate if you take common access as the baseline, which is exactly the point Proudhon and Locke fought over.

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:16

What was their "fight"?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:16

Locke (1690) argued that mixing your labor with unowned land makes it legitimately yours, so long as "enough and as good" is left for others, while Proudhon (1840, *What Is Property?*) answered, 150 years later, that labor earns you its product, not the land itself, and that permanent appropriation of the commons is theft from everyone shut out. His alternative was possession: you hold what you actually occupy and use, and lose it when you stop.

What other "baseline" is there?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:18

The rival baseline is "unowned" rather than "commonly owned": if the world starts as nobody's (res nullius, Pufendorf's "negative community"), the first taker deprives no one of anything they had a right to, whereas if it starts as everyone's (res communis, "positive community"), every appropriation takes something from all the others. Locke's "given to mankind in common" was read the first way, and Proudhon insisted on the second.

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:19

Wow, what a perverted way to conceive of a baseline. The first person that "takes" suggests that "taking" is the only option. Neither Proudhon nor Locke considered sharing?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:19

Both considered it and pushed it aside: Locke treated land held "in common by compact" (like English village commons) as a local exception to his appropriation rule, and Proudhon explicitly rejected community ownership as "oppression and slavery" of the individual by the group, preferring equal individual possession. The people who did make sharing the baseline were Gerrard Winstanley's Diggers (the earth as a "common treasury," 1649) and later Kropotkin, with Elinor Ostrom documenting how real shared commons actually work.

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:21

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:22

That's Rousseau's point in 1755 almost word for word: the first man who fenced land and said "this is mine" found people simple enough to believe him, and the "right" was manufactured by the very act it's supposed to justify. The strongest reply from the other side is Nozick's, who concedes the deprivation but argues it's legitimate if the excluded end up no worse off than under the open commons, so how does your point bear on that, given it turns on whether "future enjoyment" can be compensated rather than whether anyone had a prior right?

Nozick's argument is that it's fine to be an asshole as long as no one gets hurt. Well, I think it's pretty stupid to expect no one will get hurt when assholes/deprivers are running the show.

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:25

Fair: Nozick never disputes that taking is deprivation, he only argues it's permissible, and his test is rigged in your favor's direction anyway, because he measures "no worse off" against a primitive open commons, a baseline so low that almost any enclosure passes. The usual justification for preferring deprivation to sharing is Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968), and Ostrom won a Nobel largely for showing it's wrong in practice, since communities sharing pastures, fisheries, and water have run them sustainably for centuries.

### Millard · 2026-10-17 01:26

Does Nozick ever examine the perverseness of deprivation as the standard operating principle?

### Claude · 2026-10-17 01:26

No: in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) he pokes holes in Locke's mechanism (asking why pouring your tomato juice into the sea makes the sea yours rather than just losing your juice), but he never questions exclusive ownership itself as the default, and when he later backed away from libertarianism in *The Examined Life* (1989) it was because it neglected social solidarity, not because of deprivation.
