### Claude · 2025-07-03 15:01

You're right that "sidelining" suggests something is still present but pushed aside, rather than completely eclipsed. Here are some alternatives that convey the idea of something being completely overshadowed or eliminated from consideration:

**"Focusing on method to the **obscuring** of outcome is an age-old gambit of hypocrites."**

Other strong options:

* **eclipsing** - directly matches your description of making something not factor in at all
* **negating** - implies the outcome is nullified or canceled out
* **obliterating** - suggests complete destruction of consideration for outcome
* **subsuming** - the method completely absorbs or swallows up concern for outcome
* **supplanting** - method takes the place of outcome entirely

"Obscuring" works particularly well because it suggests the method creates a kind of fog that makes the outcome invisible, while "eclipsing" directly captures your metaphor of one thing blocking out another completely.

### Millard · 2025-07-03 15:02

### Claude · 2025-07-03 15:02

Ah, you're absolutely right about the grammatical issue with "eclipse." The sentence structure makes it sound like the outcome itself is being eclipsed, when you mean the *attention to* or *consideration of* outcome is what's being eclipsed.

Here are some options that work better with your sentence structure:

**"Focusing on method to the **obscuring** of outcome is an age-old gambit of hypocrites."**

**"Focusing on method to the **exclusion** of outcome is an age-old gambit of hypocrites."**

**"Focusing on method to the **negation** of outcome is an age-old gambit of hypocrites."**

I think **"exclusion"** works particularly well here because it clearly conveys that outcome is being shut out from consideration entirely, and it flows naturally with "to the exclusion of." It's a common, well-understood phrase that captures exactly what you're describing - the method focus is so complete that outcome doesn't even factor into the equation.

### Claude · 2025-07-03 15:04

Using "obscuring," the assertion becomes quite pointed and insightful:

**"Focusing on method to the obscuring of outcome is an age-old gambit of hypocrites."**

This captures a real phenomenon - people who get so wrapped up in procedural correctness, rule-following, or appearing to do things "the right way" that they lose sight of whether their actions actually achieve anything meaningful. The word "obscuring" works well because it suggests the method creates a kind of smokescreen.

The "hypocrites" framing is sharp because it implies these people *know* they're avoiding accountability for results by hiding behind process. Think of bureaucrats who follow every protocol while ignoring whether citizens are actually helped, or activists who prioritize ideological purity over pragmatic progress.

It's a cynical but often accurate observation about how people use methodological focus as a shield against having to defend their actual impact. The phrase "age-old gambit" reinforces that this is a timeless evasion tactic rather than genuine principle.

The assertion has bite because it calls out a specific form of intellectual dishonesty that's common across politics, institutions, and personal behavior.
