There are at least four steps in any rational (key word) formulation of a set of rights. Here I’m talking about what reasonable, intelligent people must do at a minimum to arrive at anything resembling a cogent list of rights:
1.      \*\*Recognition\*\*: They must witness and/or experience real-life patterns of behavior that no one wants to be subjected to and which no one would condone others being subjected to.
2.      \*\*Abstraction\*\*: They must generalize those patterns of behavior and designate them as wrong in principle.
3.      \*\*Articulation\*\*: They must describe the goods whose enjoyment would be damaged or destroyed by these wrongs.
4.      \*\*Declaration\*\*: They must declare that each of these goods implies a universal “right” that entitles those who “have” the right to enjoy that good and be free from wrongs that would damage or destroy their enjoyment of that good.

### Claude · 2025-08-16 15:05

Bob Black's response "I am unable to follow any of these steps" is quite revealing of his philosophical position, though it's characteristically terse. Given that he wrote *The Abolition of Work* and advocates for post-leftist anarchism, his rejection likely stems from several theoretical commitments:

**On Step 1 (Recognition):** Black would probably argue that what counts as behavior "no one wants to be subjected to" is culturally constructed rather than universal. From his anarchist perspective, many things people currently accept (like wage labor) are actually harmful but normalized through social conditioning.

**On Step 2 (Abstraction):** The move from particular instances to universal principles assumes a kind of moral realism that Black likely rejects. As someone influenced by post-structuralist thought, he'd be suspicious of claims about what's "wrong in principle" - seeing these as power relations disguised as objective truths.

**On Step 3 (Articulation):** The concept of "goods" whose enjoyment can be damaged presupposes a stable notion of human nature and universal needs. Black's post-leftist position would view this as essentialist thinking that ignores how desires and needs are socially produced.

**On Step 4 (Declaration):** This is probably where his objection is strongest. Rights discourse, for Black, is inherently statist - it requires institutions to recognize, enforce, and adjudicate rights. This contradicts his anarchist rejection of all forms of systematic authority.

Your framework assumes rational consensus is possible and desirable, while Black's position suggests that rights talk is itself a form of domination that forecloses more radical possibilities for human organization. His inability to "follow" these steps isn't necessarily a failure of comprehension, but rather a principled rejection of the entire conceptual apparatus.
