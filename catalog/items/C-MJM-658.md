**Direct Answer:** The literal wording creates ambiguity that does NOT clearly limit this to post-termination scenarios.

**The Problem:**

The phrase "**hasta en tanto la vigencia del presente contrato haya culminado**" literally means "until the term of the present contract has concluded."

This grammatically grants the landlord authority to demand a new contract or vacating **during** the contract term, not after. The power exists "hasta" (until) the contract ends—implying it ceases once the contract terminates, which is the opposite of logical intent.

**Contextual Intent vs. Literal Text:**

* The preceding paragraph clearly addresses post-termination holdover (tenant staying after expiration, 50% monthly rent increases, waiver of tacit renewal rights)
* The **intent** appears to be post-termination enforcement
* The **literal Spanish** says the landlord can demand this "until" the contract ends—which would mean during, not after

**Assessment:**

This is either:

1. A drafting error (missing "una vez que" or misplaced clause)
2. Deliberately ambiguous language favoring landlord interpretation

If challenged, a landlord could argue the literal text permits demanding a new contract or vacating at any point during the lease term with only 15 days' notice. A tenant would have to argue contextual interpretation.
