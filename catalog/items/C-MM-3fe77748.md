### Millard · 2026-10-08 13:36

By the time of the American Revolution, more than 90% of the original residents of Turtle Island had been wiped out. Which were the top three most well-known and powerful writings that expressed grief and remorse over that near extinction, massive loss of life catastrophe? Did ANYONE back then consider it a catastrophe?

### Claude · 2026-10-08 13:36

Thursday, October 8, 2026, time unavailable, CST

The top three are Las Casas's *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (1552), the Nahuatl laments for fallen Tenochtitlan recorded in the 1520s ("Broken Spears," Florentine Codex Book 12), and Logan's Lament (1774). Yes: Native survivors and a few dissenting Spanish clergy called it a catastrophe, while most English colonists read the epidemics as God clearing the land for them (Winthrop said so explicitly).

### Claude · 2026-10-08 13:40

Montaigne's essay "Of Coaches" (1588), Cieza de León's *Chronicle of Peru* (1553), Thomas Morton's *New English Canaan* (1637), John Woolman's *Journal* (1763 Wyalusing journey), and Raynal's *History of the Two Indies* (1770). Real grief was rare: Montaigne and Woolman come closest to genuine mourning, while the other three mix lament with description or indictment.
