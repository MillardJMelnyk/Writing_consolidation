The recognition of ego as a defensive construct varies significantly across different psychological frameworks:

In mainstream psychology, ego defenses are well-established - going back to Freud and Anna Freud's work on unconscious psychological strategies that protect against anxiety. However, this typically frames the ego itself as necessary, with defenses being tools it uses.

### Millard · 2025-08-12 17:10

what was Freud's view On ego before he flipped his script to save his livelihood and came up with his ego, ID, superego configuration?

### Claude · 2025-08-12 17:10

In Freud's early topographical model (before 1923), the structural model was developed in response to the perceived ambiguity of the terms "conscious" and "unconscious" in his earlier topographical model. The early model had the System Conscious - conscious thought governed by the reality principle, alongside preconscious and unconscious systems.

There wasn't really a separate "ego" concept in the early work - consciousness itself was the reality-testing function. The ego/id/superego framework was introduced in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920) and refined in The Ego and the Id (1923).

So there wasn't really a "flip" - more an evolution from a simpler conscious/unconscious model to the more complex structural model. The idea that this was to "save his livelihood" would need more specific historical evidence than I can find.

### Claude · 2025-08-12 17:24

The three most convincing theorists who view ego as a defensive construct:

**1. Donald Winnicott** - His false self concept shows how individuals create "a socially acceptable façade that conceals one's true thoughts, emotions, and vulnerabilities" as "a defense mechanism, often developed early in life to shield the authentic self from external pressures".

**2. Alice Miller** - In "The Drama of the Gifted Child," she describes how children develop a "false self" that leads to "loss of the potential true self" with "fragility of self-esteem" and "enormous fear of loss of love" due to childhood trauma and parental demands.

**3. John Bradshaw** - Though not in my search results, he's the third major figure who articulated how toxic shame creates a defensive "false self" that protects against core wounds but disconnects from authentic being.

All three see the constructed ego/false self as fundamentally protective rather than authentic - a trauma response disguised as personality.

### Millard · 2025-08-12 17:25

where do Judith Herman and Lloyd deMause fit in?

### Claude · 2025-08-12 17:25

**Judith Herman** focuses on how trauma shapes defensive psychological structures, specifically critiquing Freud for "denying women's reality when he abandoned his hypothesis that his patients' symptoms were invariably caused by seduction during childhood". Her work shows how trauma survivors develop protective adaptations that can look like ego defenses but are actually survival responses to real abuse.

**Lloyd deMause** takes this further through psychohistory - analyzing "the psychological motivations of historical events" by "analyzing events in childhood and the family, especially child abuse". He documents how "the further back in history one goes, the lower the level of child care, and the more likely children are to be killed, abandoned, beaten, terrorized, and sexually abused".

Both essentially argue that what mainstream psychology calls "ego development" is actually trauma adaptation - defensive structures built to survive childhood abuse that then get pathologized rather than understood as protective responses to real harm.
