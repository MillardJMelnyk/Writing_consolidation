Is the NASB consistent in YHWH => LORD?

### Claude · 2026-08-15 17:22

Yes — the NASB consistently renders YHWH as "LORD" (all caps/small caps) throughout the Old Testament, following the same convention as the KJV, ESV, NIV, and most major English translations. This is standard practice going back to the Septuagint's use of "Kyrios" and the Latin Vulgate's "Dominus."

A few notes on consistency:

* When YHWH and Adonai appear together (Adonai YHWH), the NASB renders it "Lord GOD" — using "GOD" in small caps instead of "LORD" to avoid the awkward repetition "Lord LORD."
* The NASB (particularly the 1971/1977 edition) was actually more willing than some translations to occasionally transliterate as "Yahweh" in a few spots, but this was inconsistent across editions — later revisions (1995, 2020) moved back to "LORD" uniformly.
* Earlier editions of the NASB (1971) did use "Yahweh" in some Old Testament instances rather than "LORD," which was a notable departure — but this was reversed in the 1995 update, standardizing back to "LORD" throughout.

So: current standard NASB (1995, 2020) — yes, consistent. The original 1971 NASB had some exceptions.

what is the state of the scholarship on the significance in the Jewish writings of preference for one of the god-words over the others in each instance?

### Claude · 2026-08-15 17:24

Two separate scholarly conversations run here, often conflated by non-specialists but distinct in method and aim.

**1. Classical source criticism (Documentary Hypothesis)**

The name-switching between Elohim and YHWH was one of the original textual clues (alongside style, doublets, theology) that Jean Astruc and later Wellhausen used to argue the Pentateuch is a composite of separate sources — the "Elohist" (E) and "Yahwist" (J) being named directly after their preferred divine name, plus P (Priestly) and D (Deuteronomist). On this view, the alternation is not meaningful in itself — it's a fingerprint of authorship, not authorial intent within a single text.

This remains foundational but is no longer treated as settled. Since the 1970s-80s it's fractured into competing models: supplementary hypothesis, fragmentary hypothesis, and various neo-documentary revisions (Baruch Schwartz, Joel Baden at the more traditional end; Rolf Rendtorff, Erhard Blum pushing fragmentary/supplementary alternatives). There's no consensus "documentary hypothesis" anymore in the guild — just contested reconstructions, with genuine skepticism (especially in European scholarship) about whether clean continuous sources can be recovered at all.

**2. Literary/theological readings of the final text**

A separate and now very active line of scholarship (canonical criticism, rhetorical criticism, "final form" readings) treats the name choice as deliberate within the text as it stands, regardless of source history. Umberto Cassuto made the classic conservative case against Wellhausen precisely by arguing the alternation is stylistically and theologically motivated, not a seam between documents — e.g., Elohim used where God's relation to the cosmos/humanity-at-large is in view, YHWH where the covenant relationship with Israel specifically is in view. Genesis 1 (Elohim, creation of the world) vs. Genesis 2 (YHWH Elohim, forming humanity in relationship) is the standard example. This reading tradition has real currency now among scholars skeptical of source division, and overlaps with classical Jewish exegesis.

**3. Rabbinic/midrashic tradition** (a third, older thread, not "academic" in the modern sense but foundational for Jewish reading)

Predates all of the above by over a millennium. The standard rabbinic association, going back to Genesis Rabbah and picked up by Rashi, is: Elohim = God acting in the attribute of justice (din), YHWH = God acting in the attribute of mercy (rachamim). So Genesis 1 uses Elohim because creation is initially approached under strict justice; by Genesis 2, mercy is folded in (YHWH Elohim) because the world couldn't survive under justice alone. This reading is theological/homiletical, not historical-critical, but it's the dominant traditional Jewish framework and still gets cited seriously in Jewish biblical scholarship as an interpretive lens distinct from source-critical explanations.

**Where things stand now**: source-critical explanation of the alternation is alive but fragmented and contested at the mechanics level (which verses belong to which source, whether "sources" is even the right model); literary/synchronic explanation of the alternation as meaningful-in-context has gained ground and is taken seriously by scholars across the religious/secular divide; and the rabbinic din/rachamim reading persists as its own separate, non-competing tradition within Jewish scholarship and popular teaching.

### Claude · 2026-08-15 17:25

Restated: In any given verse, what does it mean — for the meaning of that verse — that the writer chose YHWH rather than Elohim (or vice versa)? What is signified by the choice itself, not who scholars are or how they date the text.

Here's the substantive answer:

**The core distinction is relational vs. functional.**

* **YHWH** is the personal, covenantal name — used when God is acting in relationship to Israel specifically, or to an individual, as a personal party to a covenant. It's a proper name, tied to Exodus 3 and the covenant at Sinai. Where it appears, the text is signaling: this is the God who has bound himself to *this people/person* by promise.
* **Elohim** is the generic word for deity/divine power — used when God is acting as the transcendent power behind the cosmos, or in relation to humanity/nature generally, not tied to the covenant relationship. It's closer to a title or category than a name.

**What this does to specific texts:**

* Genesis 1 (creation of the cosmos, all humanity) uses Elohim throughout — God as universal creative power, not yet in covenant with anyone in particular.
* Genesis 2 shifts to YHWH Elohim once humanity is formed and placed in relationship — the personal name enters precisely when personal relationship begins.
* Job's dialogues largely use Elohim/Shaddai when speaking of God's raw power and inscrutability, but the frame narrative and Job's climactic encounter (Job 38-42) shift to YHWH — the personal name marking the moment God actually speaks to Job as a party, not just a force to be theorized about.
* In Ecclesiastes, YHWH never appears at all — only Elohim — which fits the book's stance: God observed as distant, impersonal, inscrutable from a "under the sun" vantage point, deliberately withholding the covenant name.

**On your Proverbs 16:9 example specifically:** YHWH is used because the verse isn't making an abstract theological claim about divine providence in general — it's asserting that the covenant God, the one in personal relationship with the reader, is the one directing the steps. Swap in Elohim and the verse becomes a more generic claim about "a god" or "deity" governing outcomes; YHWH makes it personal and covenantal — this specific God, who has committed himself to you, not fate or providence-in-the-abstract.

So the operative principle across instances: **YHWH signals covenant/personal relationship; Elohim signals generic divine power/transcendence.** The choice in any verse is functioning as a lens the writer is putting on that particular claim about God.

No survey exists quantifying scholars into percentages on this. That figure doesn't exist — I won't invent one.

What does exist, by actual weight of citation in the field:

**1. Source-critical (Documentary Hypothesis) — dominant in critical/academic biblical studies.** Name choice reflects different authorial sources (J uses YHWH, E uses Elohim, P uses Elohim then YHWH after Exodus 6), not an intentional literary device within a single author's text. Choice = fingerprint of composition history, not meaning.

**2. Rabbinic/traditional Jewish reading — dominant in Jewish exegetical tradition (Talmud, Rashi, standard yeshiva teaching).** Elohim = God acting through *din* (strict justice); YHWH = God acting through *rachamim* (mercy). Adonai = God as sovereign master, invoked in address/prayer. This is the operative framework in essentially all traditional Jewish commentary and is what's taught as "the" answer in Orthodox and most Conservative contexts.

**3. Literary/final-form (Cassuto and successors) — significant minority position, growing since the 1940s, treats the alternation as authorial and meaningful in the text as it stands: Elohim for God's relation to the cosmos/humanity generally, YHWH for the covenant relationship with Israel/individuals specifically.** Overlaps with #2 in outcome but argues from literary structure, not rabbinic tradition per se.

These three are the entirety of the field — no fourth serious contender. #1 and #2/#3 are not competing on the same axis: #1 answers "why does this word appear here" historically (who wrote it), #2/#3 answer it literarily/theologically (what does it mean that it's here). A scholar can hold #1 and still use #2/#3 to explain the final text's effect.

### Claude · 2026-08-15 17:33

**YHWH** — the personal name. Signals God acting as a party in relationship: the one who has bound himself by covenant, who speaks, promises, and is addressed by name. When a verse uses YHWH, the claim is about *this* God in his commitment to Israel or to the individual, not about deity in general. Proverbs 16:9 with YHWH says the covenant God directs your steps; with Elohim it would be a claim about providence as such.

**Elohim** — the generic word for deity, used as a title. Signals God in his capacity as power: creator, cosmic sovereign, judge over all peoples. Where the text uses Elohim, the frame is God's relation to the world and humanity at large rather than to a covenant partner. In rabbinic reading, this is the attribute of strict justice (*din*) — God as the one who orders and judges. It's also the word that can take a genuine plural ("gods," other nations' deities, and by extension human judges), so its baseline sense is *category of divine power*, not identity.

**El** — the ancient Semitic word for god, singular. Almost never neutral in the Hebrew Bible; it carries archaic, elevated, and usually compound force (El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Roi, El Olam). When El appears alone it tends to be in poetry, in older strata, or in contexts stressing might and transcendence — the sheer godness of God. Its selection marks solemnity and antiquity of diction; it also carries the resonance of the high god of the wider Canaanite world, so texts using El are often asserting that Israel's God *is* the God everyone else was reaching for.

**Eloah** — the singular of Elohim, rare (roughly 60 occurrences, over 40 of them in Job). Signals poetic register and, characteristically, God approached as a problem or an interlocutor from a distance — the God one argues with, appeals to, or contemplates rather than the covenant God who has already spoken. Its density in Job is the point: Job's friends and Job himself use Eloah while theorizing about God; YHWH appears when God actually shows up.

**Adonai** — "my Lord/Master," a title of address. Signals the speaker's posture: submission, petition, acknowledgment of authority over oneself. It shows up characteristically in prayer and direct address ("Adonai YHWH, what will you give me" — Abraham; Isaiah 6). When a text uses Adonai the emphasis falls on God's mastery and the speaker's servanthood — the relationship viewed from below. Note also that Adonai became the *spoken substitute* for YHWH, so in reading tradition it overlays the personal name, but in the written text its choice is deliberate and means lordship.

**Adon** — the ordinary word for master/lord, used for human masters, kings, and husbands throughout. When applied to God it borrows exactly that force: God as owner and master of the household or the earth ("Adon of all the earth," Joshua 3:11). Its selection places God in the frame of proprietorship and legitimate authority over what belongs to him — the same relation a servant has to a master, applied upward.

**How the choices work together:** the pairs are the interpretive payload. *YHWH Elohim* (Gen 2-3) fuses the personal name with the cosmic title — the covenant God *is* the creator. *Adonai YHWH* is address plus name — submission to the one who has promised. *El Shaddai* is archaic might plus the covenant promise of fertility to the patriarchs. In rabbinic terms the master key is Elohim = justice, YHWH = mercy, and combinations signal the two attributes acting together.

### Claude · 2026-08-15 17:43

No. His words come to us in Greek, which uses Kyrios throughout — the Septuagint's rendering for both YHWH and Adonai, so the underlying Hebrew word is not recoverable.

The two nearest things:

**The cry from the cross** (Mark 15:34 / Matt 27:46) — the only preserved instance of Jesus addressing God in Semitic words: *Eloi, Eloi* (Mark, Aramaic) / *Eli, Eli* (Matthew, Hebrew). That's El/Elohi, not Adonai.

**Psalm 110:1 quoted** (Matt 22:44 and parallels) — "The LORD said to my Lord." The Hebrew behind it is *YHWH* said to *adoni* (my lord — the possessive form of adon, not Adonai). But he's quoting, and it comes through as Kyrios/Kyrios in Greek, which is precisely why the argument he builds on it works in Greek at all.
