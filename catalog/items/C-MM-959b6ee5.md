### Millard · 2026-06-21 12:45

What are the three most well-known Israeli leaders that participated in terrorism prior to the formation of Israel?

### Claude · 2026-06-21 12:45

Menachem Begin, Yitzhak Shamir, and Ariel Sharon are typically cited.

* **Menachem Begin** — led Irgun (Etzel), responsible for the 1946 King David Hotel bombing and the 1948 Deir Yassin massacre. Later became Israel's 6th Prime Minister and co-signed the Egypt-Israel peace treaty.
* **Yitzhak Shamir** — a leader of Lehi (the "Stern Gang"), involved in the 1944 assassination of Lord Moyne (British Minister of State for the Middle East) and linked to the 1948 assassination of UN mediator Folke Bernadotte. Later became Israel's 7th Prime Minister.
* **Ariel Sharon** — participated in Unit 101's 1953 Qibya raid, which killed dozens of Palestinian civilians; this falls right around statehood (1948) rather than strictly before it, so some lists swap him for others. Later became the 11th Prime Minister, known for the 1982 Sabra and Shatila massacre during the Lebanon War (though that's well after independence and is its own separate controversy).

Worth noting: whether these groups' actions count as "terrorism" versus "resistance/insurgency against British rule" is itself contested — it depends on definitions (intentional targeting of civilians vs. military/state targets) and political framing. Begin's and Shamir's organizations were officially designated terrorist organizations by the British Mandate authorities at the time, which is the strongest basis for the label.

### Millard · 2026-06-21 12:47

Why did Great Britain give in to Zionist demands?

### Claude · 2026-06-21 12:47

Britain's withdrawal from Palestine and the events leading to partition came from several converging pressures, not one single cause:

**Military and economic exhaustion.** Britain emerged from WWII financially devastated and overstretched, struggling to hold onto an empire it could no longer afford to police. Palestine required tens of thousands of troops to administer at a moment when Britain was also juggling India, Greece, and other crises.

**Zionist paramilitary pressure.** Groups like Irgun and Lehi waged an insurgency against British forces and installations — the King David Hotel bombing being the most prominent example — aiming to make continued British rule too costly to sustain. There's real historical debate about how decisive this was versus other factors, but it was a constant drain on British resources and morale.

**International and American pressure.** The US, especially under Truman, pushed hard for increased Jewish immigration into Palestine after the Holocaust, and later supported partition at the UN. Britain depended heavily on American loans and goodwill post-war, giving Washington real leverage.

**Moral weight of the Holocaust.** The scale of the Holocaust shifted global and British public opinion considerably, making the existing restrictive immigration policy (the 1939 White Paper limits) harder to defend, especially with displaced persons camps full of Jewish refugees in Europe.

**UN involvement.** Britain ultimately punted the question to the United Nations in 1947, which voted for partition. Britain didn't actively choose to "give in" so much as declare it could no longer govern the Mandate and withdrew by May 1948, after which Israel declared independence and the surrounding Arab states intervened militarily.

Historians disagree on the relative weight of these factors — some emphasize the insurgency, others emphasize British war-weariness and US pressure as more decisive. It's not a settled question with one clean answer.

### Millard · 2026-06-21 12:49

What participation was there by Palestinians in the events that led up to the Balfour declaration?

### Claude · 2026-06-21 12:49

Very little direct participation, and that's a significant part of the historical critique of the declaration.

**No consultation occurred.** The Balfour Declaration of 1917 was a letter from British Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour to Lord Rothschild, expressing British support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people." Palestinian Arabs — who made up roughly 90% of the population at the time — were not consulted, represented, or even directly named in the document. They appear only obliquely as "the existing non-Jewish communities," whose "civil and religious rights" (not political or national rights) were to be protected.

**No organized Palestinian national movement yet existed in the same form.** Palestinian Arab identity as a distinct national movement was still forming during this period. There were local and regional political structures, Ottoman-era notable families, and a broader Arab nationalist movement (centered partly around Sharif Hussein and the Arab Revolt against the Ottomans), but no unified Palestinian Arab body negotiating with Britain the way Zionist organizations were.

**Zionist diplomatic activity, by contrast, was extensive.** Chaim Weizmann and others lobbied British officials directly for years, building relationships with figures like Balfour, Lloyd George, and Mark Sykes (of Sykes-Picot).

**Competing British promises complicate the picture.** Britain had also given assurances to Arab leaders during WWII — most notably through the McMahon-Hussein correspondence (1915-16), which seemed to promise support for Arab independence in exchange for revolt against the Ottomans. Whether Palestine was included in that promised territory is itself disputed and was a major source of Arab grievance once the Balfour Declaration became public.

**Reaction came after, not before.** Palestinian Arab political organizing — petitions, delegations to London, the 1920s-30s Arab Higher Committee, and eventually the 1936-39 Arab Revolt — emerged largely in response to the declaration and subsequent British Mandate policy, not as participants in shaping it.

So the short answer: essentially none in the decision itself. The declaration was made by a colonial power, in consultation with a non-resident national movement, about the future of a land whose actual inhabitants had no seat at the table — which is precisely why it remains so contested.
