Taking "this" as the last paragraph — the inventory and how defects get allocated between you and the landlord. If you meant something else, say so.

There are three separate mechanisms in this contract for putting a defect on someone's side of the ledger. They operate at different times and under different standards, and they interact.

**Mechanism 1 — Annex 2, the inventory, at handover**

Clause 7 says the unit is received "en estado de servir para el uso convenido," with all accessories and complete installations, "cuyo inventario se agrega como ANEXO 2," and you are obliged to return it "en la forma y con los bienes en que fue recibido."

That sentence does two things. It creates a baseline — whatever Annex 2 says is in the unit is what you owe back — and it embeds a legal presumption: by signing, you declare the unit was in serviceable condition when you took it.

The practical consequence: anything broken, stained, cracked, leaking or missing on day one that isn't written into Annex 2 is presumed to have happened on your watch. At move-out the landlord doesn't have to prove you caused it. You have to prove you didn't. And Clause 1 compounds this — it states the unit "se encuentra en perfectas condiciones." You're signing a document that says the apartment was perfect when you got it.

This is the single largest deposit risk in the contract, and it's resolved entirely before you move in, not after. Annex 2 wasn't provided to you. You cannot evaluate it. Do not sign until you've walked the unit with the inventory in hand and written every existing defect onto it, with photographs, before signature.

**Mechanism 2 — Clause 6 bis, the 48-hour rule, for habitability defects**

Scope: "desperfectos o defectos, que puedan dejar el inmueble en un estado de inhabitabilidad." Serious failures — water, electrical, gas, structural, drainage, anything that makes the place unlivable.

If the defect wasn't caused by you or your occupants, the repair is the landlord's. But the clause conditions that: you must notify in writing within 48 hours of detection, "so pena de pagar los daños y perjuicios que cause con su omisión."

Read that penalty precisely. It doesn't transfer the whole repair to you. It makes you liable for the *incremental* damage your delay caused. A pipe leaking behind a wall is still the landlord's pipe; the floor that rotted because you waited two weeks to mention it is yours. In practice the landlord will argue for the broader reading, and you'll be arguing over an unwritten counterfactual.

Once you notify, the landlord's clock starts: fifteen calendar days to *begin* repairs — not finish — except for evidently urgent ones, which are immediate. "Evidentemente urgentes" is undefined.

Valid notice is email or WhatsApp to the contact details in Clause 18, but only "siempre que dichos avisos sean debidamente recibidos por su destinatario." Received, not sent. This is why the blank landlord email is not a clerical detail. WhatsApp gives you delivery and read receipts; email to a blank address gives you nothing. Use WhatsApp, screenshot the double blue checks, and follow up by email once you have the address.

**Mechanism 3 — Clause 7, "oportunamente," for everything else**

For ordinary faults that don't threaten habitability, Clause 7 says only that you must "informar oportunamente" — timely notice, no fixed deadline.

This is the clause that protects your deposit, and it's the most useful one in the contract. It states that faults timely reported which derive from natural wear, age or exhaustion of useful life, and which aren't attributable to you, "no podrán ser considerados como daños a su cargo al momento de la entrega." Report it, and it cannot be charged to you at move-out. Clause 13 repeats this in nearly identical terms.

The corollary: an unreported defect keeps no such protection. Silence for twelve months about a slowly failing water heater means that at handover it's an undocumented change from the "perfect condition" baseline.

**How the allocation actually works**

Clause 7 splits it as: minor repairs, ordinary maintenance and anything from improper use, negligence or lack of maintenance by you, your occupants, visitors or third parties you admit — yours. Major or general repairs, and replacement of installations, systems, equipment or components failing from exhausted useful life, age, expiry, inherent defect or natural deterioration — the landlord's, provided your acts or omissions didn't cause the need.

Then the tiebreaker: "Cuando exista duda respecto de la causa de un desperfecto, deberá atenderse a su naturaleza, estado, antigüedad y, cuando resulte necesario, a la valoración o diagnóstico técnico correspondiente."

This is a genuinely good clause and someone negotiated for it. It means a disputed item gets decided by the age and nature of the component, with a technical diagnosis if needed — not by the landlord's assertion. A ten-year-old boiler that dies is an age failure regardless of what the landlord claims. But it only engages if you have a record establishing the component's prior state. Without documentation, "naturaleza, estado, antigüedad" is just the landlord's word.

**What this means operationally**

Everything above collapses into a documentation problem. The contract gives you real protections — the age-based tiebreaker, the reported-defects carve-out, the photo-and-receipt requirement on deposit deductions in Clause 12 — but every one of them is conditioned on a written record you create.

So: annotate Annex 2 exhaustively before signing and photograph the whole unit on handover day, including meter readings. Report anything you notice the day you notice it, in writing, via WhatsApp, with a photo. Never verbally. Keep the thread in one conversation so it reads chronologically. For anything that could make the place unlivable, treat 48 hours as the hard limit; for everything else, report it anyway — it costs nothing and it's what converts a future deduction into a landlord obligation.

### Claude · 2026-09-11 21:36

**What extinción de dominio actually is**

It's a civil forfeiture action by the Mexican state against *property*, not against a person. The FGR sues the asset itself. Because it's civil and autonomous, it runs independently of any criminal case — nobody has to be convicted, and the death of the person under investigation doesn't extinguish the action; it survives against heirs and successors. The governing statute is the Ley Nacional de Extinción de Dominio, last reformed 28 November 2025 and still in force in 2026. It's the implementing law for Article 22 of the Constitution and covers organized crime, drug offenses, kidnapping, human trafficking, fuel theft, corruption, extortion, vehicle theft and concealment.

The property is taken from the owner. The owner gets nothing. If the apartment is forfeited, Laura Daniela Gomez Meza loses an asset worth several million pesos.

**Why the landlord cares, and what the law gives her**

She has a defense. Good faith is presumed, but to claim the presumption the affected party must sufficiently establish it — she has to show she vetted the tenant and had no knowledge of illicit activity. That's precisely why Declarations I(d), I(f), II(f), II(g) and II(h) exist. They're not filler. They're her evidentiary file. When she stands in front of a forfeiture judge, she produces this contract and says: I asked, he swore under oath, I acted in good faith.

So a large part of this machinery exists to protect her from the state. That part is reasonable and you shouldn't fight it.

**What the contract does on top of that**

Four clauses stack, and they don't do the same thing.

*Clause 4* — if you use the property for something other than habitational and forfeiture results, you indemnify her "la cantidad que resulte de aplicar el valor de venta que tuviere la propiedad al momento de su extinción." The market sale value of the apartment at the moment it's taken.

*Clause 15* — you indemnify and hold her harmless if forfeiture proceeds due to any crime, "sea imputable a 'EL ARRENDATARIO' o a cualquier persona que 'EL ARRENDATARIO' hubiere permitido el acceso al Inmueble."

*Clause 16* — the broadest. Automatic rescission if the property is "resguardado, relacionado, investigado o asegurado" by any authority arising from the "sospecha o comprobación" of crimes "consumados o intentados dentro o fuera del inmueble." And if the property is disposed of as a precautionary measure, you or your obligada solidaria pay "los daños y perjuicios correspondientes además del resarcimiento total del bien."

*Clause 10* — your obligada solidaria is on the hook for all of it, jointly, having waived orden, excusión and división.

**Why I called it uncapped**

Clause 4 gives one full-value indemnity. Clause 16 gives another one ("resarcimiento total del bien") *plus* damages and losses on top. Nothing in the contract says these are alternatives or that recovery is limited to one. On a literal reading you could owe the value of the apartment twice over, plus consequential damages, plus legal costs under Clause 13. Against $8,300 a month in rent, that's an exposure ratio in the hundreds.

**The triggers are looser than they should be**

Three specific problems in how it's drafted.

*Suspicion, not proof.* Clause 16 fires on "sospecha," and on the property merely being "relacionado" or "investigado." No conviction, no judgment, not even formal charges. Technically, an investigation that names the address and later goes nowhere satisfies the text.

*Outside the property.* "Dentro o fuera del inmueble." A crime committed somewhere else entirely, by you or by anyone you let in the door, that results in the apartment being linked to an investigation.

*No fault requirement.* Clause 15 covers persons you admitted "bajo su exclusiva responsabilidad," with no requirement that you knew or could have known. It's strict liability for the conduct of your guests, your plumber, your cleaner.

**How likely is any of this**

For you, effectively zero. You're a trader and an online English teacher in a residential condominium in Corregidora. You're not running cash through the apartment, not storing anything, not hosting anyone you don't know. The realistic scenarios — safe house, clandestine lab, stash location — don't touch you.

What matters isn't the probability, it's that the clause is drafted to be unlimited when it could easily be bounded, and there's no reason for you to accept unlimited exposure when a cap costs the landlord nothing in the only scenario where she'd realistically invoke it.

**What to ask for**

Push for four changes, in order of value:

1. **A cap.** A fixed peso ceiling or a multiple of annual rent. This is the one that actually matters, and it's the one they're most likely to refuse — but asking is free.
2. **Attributability.** Liability only where the forfeiture results from acts or omissions attributable to you by fault or intent. Strike strict liability for third parties you had no reason to suspect.
3. **A final judgment.** Indemnity payable only on a firm, non-appealable forfeiture ruling — not on suspicion, investigation or a precautionary measure. Rescission on mere investigation is defensible; a multi-million-peso payment obligation on mere suspicion is not.
4. **Delete "fuera del inmueble."** Limit it to conduct connected to the property.

Realistically ACCR will resist 1 and give you some version of 2 and 3, because 2 and 3 cost the landlord nothing — she still has her good-faith defense either way. Take what you get. This is a lower priority than the missing tenant name, the blank email, the pena convencional, and Annex 2.

I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice. If you want one item on this contract reviewed by a Querétaro abogado, make it this one plus Clause 13 — an hour of consult is cheap against what's written here.

### Claude · 2026-09-11 22:56

A workable one has five parts, and each closes a specific gap.

**1. Advance notice and a scheduled walkthrough.** The tenant gives written notice of the vacate date. The landlord inspects on that date or within a fixed window — say two business days. If she doesn't show within the window, the property is deemed received in the condition the tenant documented.

**2. This is what "recibir formalmente" should mean. Right now the contract uses the phrase without defining it.

**3. A settlement window for services.** The real obstacle is the one they already named — CFE and water bills arrive late. So separate handover from settlement: the acta establishes the property was received on day X, and the parties then have a defined period, say 30 days, to close out final utility bills, maintenance fees, and internet cancellation. The point is that the guarantor's liability for *possession* ends at the acta, and only her liability for *quantified debts* survives into the settlement window.

**4. Part 2 — Fortezza (Terra C-48) vs ACCR (Helios C-49)
----------------------------------------------------

### Where the ACCR contract is better

**Late rent penalties.** Fortezza escalates 10% / 20% / 30% by days late. ACCR is 5% within five days, 10% after. On a month's rent that's $830 worst case at ACCR versus $2,400 at Fortezza.

**Payment method.** Fortezza requires cash, handed over at the property, on the 1st. No automatic paper trail — you're dependent on receipts. ACCR is electronic transfer to a named HSBC account with a comprobante sent every month. Much better evidence position.

**Repair allocation.** Fortezza has one short vicios ocultos clause and nothing else. ACCR's Clause 7 splits major versus minor, assigns age-and-useful-life failures to the landlord, gives you the technical-diagnosis tiebreaker for disputed causes, and carves reported defects out of your move-out liability. That's the most tenant-favorable thing in the Helios contract and Fortezza has no equivalent.

**Deposit deductions.** ACCR requires photographic evidence plus receipts, invoices or quotes. Fortezza says nothing, meaning deductions are asserted rather than proved.

**Notices.** ACCR now accepts email and WhatsApp as written notice — the amendment you won on the 10th. Fortezza Clause 19 requires written notice with acuse de recibo, which is stricter.

**Renewal increase.** Fortezza uses 5% annual legal interest "o lo acordado." ACCR uses the INEGI INPC, which is an objective published figure and currently runs below 5%.

**Rescission for late rent.** Fortezza triggers at two months unpaid. ACCR triggers at one. Fortezza wins this one — correction to my ordering, it belongs in the next section.

### Where the ACCR contract is worse

**Rescission for arrears.** Fortezza: two or more months. ACCR: one month or more. Half the runway.

**Repair start time.** Fortezza gives the landlord 5 calendar days from written notice to begin. ACCR gives 15. Three times slower, and this is exactly the failure mode you described with the bathroom.

**Death of the tenant.** Fortezza handles it humanely — contract terminates early with no penalty, occupants get 15 calendar days to hand over. ACCR lists it as a bare rescission ground, 16(N), with no grace period and no express penalty waiver. Given the age difference between the two tenants this may matter more on paper than in practice, but Fortezza's drafting is plainly better.

**Landlord-side penalty.** Fortezza Clause 12, second paragraph: if the landlord terminates unilaterally without tenant breach, she pays one month's rent or gives 30 days rent-free. The ACCR contract has no equivalent anywhere — every penalty runs one direction. That's the single clearest asymmetry between the two documents.

**Handover.** Fortezza Clause 10 requires the return to be documented "mediante carta-entrega firmando EL ARRENDADOR de conformidad" — a signed handover letter is a contractual obligation. ACCR has nothing. Arlene said they do an acta in practice, but Fortezza put it in the contract.

**Guarantor exposure.** Fortezza Clause 17 waives orden and exclusión. ACCR Clause 10 waives orden, excusión *and* división, and adds that her liability survives any renewal, modification or indulgence without notice to her or her consent. Claudia is materially more exposed under the Helios contract than under the one she already signed for your dad.

**The landlord's notification address.** Fortezza Declaration 1.D gives a real street address. ACCR leaves it blank. Same agency-quality point I flagged earlier, now with a direct comparison.

**Early termination penalty scope.** Fortezza ties the 2-month penalty specifically to early vacating. ACCR Clause 13 attaches it to breach of any clause. This is the one Kelly is still fighting, and the Fortezza contract is the proof that the narrower version is normal in this market.
