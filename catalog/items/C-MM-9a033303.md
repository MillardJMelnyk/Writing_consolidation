### Claude · 2026-08-16 13:21

Mostly nonsense, and one claim is chemically impossible.

**The pH claim is false.** Sodium bicarbonate can't push water past about 8.3 — it's a buffer, that's its ceiling. Magnesium chloride in solution is slightly *acidic* and pulls it back down further. You'd land around pH 7–8.5. Anything actually at pH 12–13 would be drain cleaner and would burn your mouth and esophagus.

**"Alkalizing the body" isn't a thing.** Blood pH is held at 7.35–7.45 by your lungs and kidneys. Stomach acid neutralizes the bicarbonate before it goes anywhere. You change urine pH, nothing else.

**The surface tension part is invented.** Dissolved salts *raise* surface tension. Water molecules don't "break apart" to enter capillaries.

**What you'd actually be drinking:** 30 mL/day delivers roughly 60–125 mg of magnesium (depending on whether the MgCl₂ is anhydrous or hexahydrate) plus about 50 mg of sodium. That's a small magnesium supplement — around a quarter of a day's requirement. Nothing more.

Pink salt is ~98% sodium chloride; the trace minerals are present in amounts too small to matter.

The one claim with partial footing is kidney stones — urinary alkalinization helps *some* stone types and hurts others. Not a general benefit.

Main real risk: anyone with reduced kidney function shouldn't be loading magnesium.

### Claude · 2026-08-16 14:25

Iodized salt. That's the gap nobody flags.

If you switched to pink Himalayan or sea salt, you dropped iodine — those aren't iodized, and Mexican table salt is. Iodine deficiency causes hypothyroidism, which causes exactly the fatigue you're describing. **Selenium** is the other one. Sardines cover it. So do Brazil nuts — two a day is a full day's requirement, and more than four is too many.

On the vitamin C and spinach together: that pairing is the best thing you can do for iron absorption. Spinach iron is the poorly-absorbed kind, and vitamin C in the same meal roughly triples uptake. Take the powder with your beans and lentils too, same reason.

Two cautions on the powder, though.

Keep it to about half a gram a day. Your body dumps everything past that, and high doses convert to oxalate in the urine — which is a kidney stone risk. Spinach is already one of the highest-oxalate foods there is. A kilogram of ascorbic acid invites large scoops; don't.

Cook the spinach rather than eating it raw. Boiling and draining cuts the oxalate substantially and lets you eat more of it.

After spinach, eggs, sardines, and pepitas, your diet has no obvious holes left. The fruit gap is mostly potassium and polyphenols, and avocado and beans have the potassium covered.

Two schedules exist. The conventional one and the hyperbolic one, and they disagree most at the bottom.

**Conventional (what most prescribers use):**  
100 → 75 → 50 → 25 → 0, two to four weeks per step.

**Hyperbolic (Horowitz & Taylor, Lancet Psychiatry 2019):**  
Serotonin transporter occupancy against dose is a hyperbolic curve, not a line. Rough occupancy figures for sertraline:

| Dose | SERT occupancy |
| --- | --- |
| 100 mg | ~85% |
| 50 mg | ~80% |
| 25 mg | ~70% |
| 12.5 mg | ~60% |
| 5 mg | ~40% |
| 2 mg | ~20% |
| 1 mg | ~10% |

Dropping 100 → 75 removes almost nothing pharmacologically. Dropping 5 → 0 removes 40 percentage points in one step. That's why the conventional schedule feels easy at the top and falls apart at the end — it's the same milligram step doing wildly different work.

The hyperbolic schedule reduces by a constant fraction of the *current* dose, not a constant number of milligrams:

100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6 → 3 → 1.5 → 0.75 → stop

Four weeks minimum per step. Roughly 25% of current dose per step; 10% if you want it slower.

Three problems.
