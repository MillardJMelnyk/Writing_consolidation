### Claude · 2025-12-10 17:41

No, not basically. The paper does state that processes have been "black-boxed," but that's a secondary observation, not the main argument.

**What it actually says:**

The paper explicitly notes (introduction): "Given these difficulties, it is no wonder that the processes of information use largely tend to remain 'blackboxed' in information studies."

**But the main argument is different:**

The paper's purpose is to analyze two alternative frameworks—Cook and Brown's "epistemic work" and Orlikowski's "knowing in practice"—that specifically challenge the black-box problem by reconceptualizing information use as embedded in *action and practice*, not as an internal cognitive process.

**The actual diagnosis:**

The real target is cognitive constructivism's assumption that information use is primarily "a cognitive process occurring within the mind of an individual" (Research Design section). The paper argues this approach treats information as separate from doing, when it should be understood as inseparable from situated action.

**The explicit conclusion:**

"The conceptions of epistemic work and knowing in practice provide holistic frameworks for the study of information use in context, while *not probing the details of information use processes per se*."

So the paper acknowledges the black-box problem exists, but proposes a different solution: shift focus from *internal cognitive processing* to *contextualized practice*—not to better illuminate the cognitive details, but to bypass the question entirely by reframing what information use is.

### Millard · 2025-12-10 18:20

### Claude · 2025-12-10 18:20

Correct. Your use is epistemologically grounded—the work of validating/justifying claims to truth. Theirs is practice-grounded—how knowledge and knowing operate *within* action.

The fundamental difference: You're asking "how do we establish what's true?" Their question is "how does knowing happen when people act?"

Your epistemic work is about *process of knowing* (verification, confirmation, testing against reality). Theirs is about *knowing as process* (embedded in doing).

These don't align. Cook and Brown explicitly move away from traditional epistemology—they reject what they call "epistemology of possession" (knowledge as static truth-claims in the head) in favor of "epistemology of practice" (knowing as enacted relation to the world).

Your framework prioritizes the truth-ascertainment dimension. Theirs brackets the question of truth-validation entirely and focuses on how knowing manifests in situated action.

This matters for your codependence/solity work: if you're examining how consciousness relates to truth-access (substrate-grounded vs. mediated/referential), you're asking something epistemologically prior to what they're asking. You're asking "what conditions make accurate knowing possible?" They're asking "how does knowing-in-action work, regardless of its truth-value?"

### Claude · 2025-12-10 18:23

That's sharp. You're proposing the epistemological problem itself is *generated by* the knowing-doing split, not discovered as a pre-existing fact.

The logic unfolds this way:

**The split creates the problem:** Once you treat knowledge as a separable entity (possession model), you immediately get: "How do we ensure this possession maps to reality?" The gap between knower and world *requires* validation mechanisms.

**But if the split is illegitimate...** then you don't have knowledge *about* reality; you have knowing *as part of* reality-engagement. The validation question dissolves because there's no gap to bridge.

**This connects directly to your codependence/solity framework:**

Referential consciousness (mediated by external authority) *requires* the epistemological apparatus because it's working at a distance from substrate-grounded reality. The subject can't directly access truth; it needs validation systems. Epistemology becomes necessary infrastructure.

But substrate-grounded solity consciousness would have no such gap. Knowing and doing wouldn't be separate problems requiring reconciliation—they'd be aspects of the same phenomena.

**Embodied wisdom**, then, isn't a *different answer* to the epistemological question. It's operating in a frame where the question never properly formed because the premise (separation of knower from known) was never accepted.

The question: Is epistemology solving a real problem, or excavating a problem created by a false ontology?

That reframes what your "truth-ascertainment work" actually is.

### Millard · 2025-12-10 18:25

how does this relate to the work of Cook and Brown?

### Claude · 2025-12-10 18:25

Cook and Brown are partially inside and partially outside your insight.

**They escape part of the trap:**

They correctly reject epistemology of possession (the illusion that knowledge is a static thing you possess and must then apply to action). They insist knowing *is* part of action, not separate from it.

**But they don't escape fully:**

They still maintain knowledge and knowing as *distinct* entities that must be reconciled. They say both are necessary, each "does work the others cannot"—explicit and tacit knowledge are "tools at the service of knowing."

This means they're still operating within the structure of the problem. They're trying to integrate knowledge and knowing, but they haven't questioned whether the distinction itself is an artifact of the schism.

**Your deeper claim would be:**

In substrate-grounded consciousness, you wouldn't have "knowledge" and "knowing" as separable categories requiring integration. The distinction wouldn't arise. There would be no need to theorize how they "dance together" because they'd never have been split.

Cook and Brown have rehabilitated practice and action into the picture—crucial move. But they're still saying: "Here's knowledge (explicit/tacit, possessed), here's knowing (enacted in action), now see how they interrelate?"

Your framework would say: once you're genuinely grounded in substrate, the question "how do knowledge and knowing relate?" becomes incoherent. There's just *knowing-in-being*.

So Cook and Brown have moved closer to the legitimate ontology, but they're still operating within the referential structure—bridging gaps rather than dissolving the premise that gaps exist.
