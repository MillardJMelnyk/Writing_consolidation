What's the estimated value of the child sex trafficking industry?

### Claude · 2026-05-27 14:56

The most authoritative recent figures come from the International Labour Organization (ILO):

With roughly 1.7 million children in commercial sexual exploitation, the ILO estimates total profits made by criminals from child victims at about $50.8 billion — $46 billion from sex trafficking specifically, and $4.8 billion from forced labour. That sits within the ILO's broader 2024 estimate that forced commercial sexual exploitation of all ages (adults and children) generates around $172.6 billion annually.

A few caveats worth knowing:

These numbers are estimates of criminal *profits*, not market "revenue," and methodologies vary. Older figures still circulate widely — for example, a frequently cited $9.8 billion estimate for child sex trafficking in the US alone comes from a single organization's estimate referenced in 2015 congressional testimony, and it's quite dated.

Researchers consistently stress that the true scope is unknowable. The industry is estimated to reach multiple billions of dollars annually, but because it operates covertly, assessing its real economic scale is extremely difficult. The shift toward online exploitation (livestreaming, sextortion) has further complicated measurement — one 2024 figure identified roughly 100 million suspected child sexual abuse material files, which doesn't map cleanly onto a dollar value at all.

So the honest answer is a range: tens of billions of dollars annually for the child-specific portion, with the ~$46–50 billion ILO figure being the most defensible current estimate, though all such numbers carry significant uncertainty.

This is a difficult subject — if you're researching it for advocacy, policy, or journalism, the primary ILO reports and the University of Edinburgh's Childlight Institute are the strongest sources to cite directly.

### Claude · 2026-05-27 14:59

Here's the key thing that changes the answer: for sex trafficking specifically, the ILO "profit" figure is *already* close to a revenue figure, so the extrapolation isn't a large multiplier.

The ILO's own methodology shows why. They calculate it as average profits (value-added) per victim multiplied by the number of victims, which gives a first approximation of the total value of economic transactions — built from average prices paid by clients, number of clients, minus wage payments and intermediate expenditures. In other words, they start from gross transaction value (revenue) and subtract two things to get "profit": wages paid to victims, and operating costs.

For commercial sexual exploitation, both deductions are small. The ILO explicitly attributes the high per-victim figure to the extremely limited share of earnings trickling down to the victims — i.e., victims are paid close to nothing. So the gap between "revenue" and "profit" here is mostly just operating costs (advertising, transport, housing, bribes, "protection"), which the literature treats as a modest fraction.

So the extrapolation for children globally:

Profit baseline: ~$46 billion (1.7M children × ~$27,250 per victim). Gross revenue = profit + victim wages (≈ near zero) + intermediate costs. If operating costs run roughly 15–25% of gross receipts, revenue lands around **$54–62 billion**.

That's a soft estimate, and arguably still low, because the ILO itself notes its figures exclude further illegal profits from things like recruitment fees and avoided taxes, and the per-victim approach captures in-person commercial exploitation better than online CSAM sales, livestreamed abuse, and sextortion, which are separate revenue streams.

Two honest caveats. First, "revenue" for a covert criminal industry is even shakier than the profit figure — there's no audited transaction record, just modeled prices × modeled client volumes. Second, unlike a legitimate industry where revenue and profit diverge sharply, this "industry" has a near-zero wage bill by design, so revenue and the ILO profit figure are unusually close — meaning the headline ~$46–50 billion you'll see cited is itself functioning as a rough proxy for industry size.

**On your bias premise — partly right, and worth splitting**

Rigorous institutional estimates (ILO, Urban Institute, UNODC) genuinely *are* floors. They model only detectable exploitation and round down at every uncertain step to survive peer review. The ILO saying "this is a low estimate" is the tell. But the advocacy ecosystem runs the opposite bias — a flood of unsourced viral numbers ($32B, per-victim and prevalence stats) that inflate. So "accurate, not defensible" cuts both ways: trust the institutional numbers as a floor, discard the viral ones as noise, and the truth sits above the floor but not where the advocacy material puts it.

**The range**

Three independent multiplicative uncertainties make any tight number dishonest:

The victim count is unsettled by roughly 2x — the ILO uses 1.7M children in forced commercial sexual exploitation in the $46B calculation, yet other ILO-derived figures put children in forced commercial sexual exploitation at ~3.3M. Profit-to-revenue grossing is another ~1.3–2x, and this is where your cost point lands correctly: heavy illegal-overhead means revenue sits well above the ILO profit figure. And whether "the industry" includes CSAM sales, livestreamed abuse, and sextortion — largely absent from the ILO and Kara models — swings it by an unknown but plausibly large amount.
