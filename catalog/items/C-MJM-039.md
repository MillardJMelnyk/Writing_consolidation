### Millard · 2025-06-11 16:00

What are the core definitional characteristics of violence, meaning those that set it apart from everything else, without which an event would not be violent, but with which we cannot deny that it is violent?

### Claude · 2025-06-11 16:01

Violence, at its definitional core, requires the **intentional use of physical force against another being that causes or threatens harm**. Let me break down these essential characteristics:

**Physical force** is the foundational element - violence necessarily involves bodily action or the deployment of physical power. This distinguishes it from purely psychological manipulation, economic coercion, or structural oppression, which may be harmful but aren't violent in the strict sense.

**Intentionality** separates violence from accidents. A person struck by a falling tree isn't a victim of violence, but someone struck by a deliberately thrown rock is. The actor must mean to apply force, even if they don't intend all the consequences that follow.

**Directedness against another being** means violence requires a target - typically a person, though we extend the concept to animals. Self-harm occupies an ambiguous space, as does destruction of inanimate objects (which we might call "violent" metaphorically).

**Harm or threat of harm** provides violence with its moral weight and social significance. Force that causes no damage and poses no credible threat - like a gentle tap - fails to meet our intuitive understanding of violence.

These elements work together as necessary conditions. Remove any one, and we lose what makes an act distinctively violent. A surgeon uses physical force intentionally against another person, but without intent to harm. A person accidentally injures someone through physical contact, but lacks intention. Psychological abuse may intentionally harm another, but lacks the physical force component.

This definition captures everything from a punch to warfare while excluding accidents, consensual activities, and non-physical forms of harm that we might describe as "violent" only metaphorically.

But this isn't actually arbitrary; it reflects a fundamental distinction we make across all human actions. Consider: handing someone a $20 bill as a gift versus handing them the same bill as payment for drugs. Physically identical acts, but we classify them entirely differently based on intent.

The distinction matters because violence isn't just about physical damage - it's about the social and moral phenomenon of one agent deliberately harming another. Intent distinguishes accidents from attacks, manslaughter from murder, medical procedures from assault. Without this distinction, we'd have no coherent basis for moral judgment, legal responsibility, or social response to harmful acts.

The "arbitrariness" you point to is actually the unavoidable line between things that happen to us and things that are done to us. That line - marked by intent - is what makes violence a distinctly human phenomenon rather than just another form of physical damage.

### Claude · 2025-06-11 16:18

Yes, that's a powerful way to frame it. Attack captures the core element across all these forms - it's an aggressive action directed against something's integrity or wholeness.

The wrecking ball attacks the building's structure. Gaslighting attacks someone's grip on reality. Defamation attacks their reputation and social standing. Physical assault attacks their bodily integrity. Lies attack their understanding of truth.

"Attack" encompasses both the forceful, aggressive nature and the directedness against a target. It doesn't require consciousness in the victim (the house is attacked by the wrecking ball) or intent in the agent (Pompeii was attacked by the volcano). It just requires aggressive force directed against something in a way that damages or destroys it.

So violence could be: **any attack that damages or destroys its target's integrity**.

This unifies all the forms we've discussed under one concept while avoiding the problems with definitions based on intent, consciousness, or purely physical force.
