### Millard · 2025-08-16 20:05

In my book (draft), I repeat the phrase, "evildoing, violation, and harm" because I can't think of a fitting, impactful word that covers them all. Any ideas?

### Claude · 2025-08-16 20:05

Here are some powerful single words that could capture "evildoing, violation, and harm":

**Transgression** - This has a strong moral weight and covers crossing boundaries, violating principles, and causing harm through wrongdoing.

**Malefaction** - A formal but impactful term that literally means "evil deed" but encompasses the broader spectrum of harmful wrongdoing.

**Depredation** - Suggests destructive harm and violation, with connotations of predatory behavior.

**Iniquity** - Carries deep moral condemnation and suggests both the evil intent and harmful consequences.

**Malfeasance** - Originally legal terminology, but powerful for describing intentional wrongdoing that causes harm.

**Desecration** - If your context involves something sacred or valued being violated/harmed, this adds emotional weight.

**Atrocity** - For more severe contexts, this single word conveys the full spectrum of evil, violation, and harm.

The choice depends on your book's tone and context. "Transgression" might work well for philosophical or literary contexts, while "atrocity" suits more dramatic scenarios. "Iniquity" has a timeless, almost biblical weight that could be very effective.

What's the general tone and subject matter of your book? That might help narrow down which would resonate most powerfully with your intended meaning.

### Claude · 2025-08-16 20:06

For something on the level of "atrocity," consider these powerful options:

**Abomination** - Carries deep moral revulsion and suggests something fundamentally wrong and harmful. Has weight without being overused.

**Enormity** - Often misused to mean "large size," but its true meaning is "extreme evil or wickedness." Very impactful when used correctly.

**Barbarity** - Suggests cruel, savage wrongdoing that violates civilized standards. Strong moral condemnation built in.

**Savagery** - Raw, brutal harm and violation. Visceral impact.

**Brutality** - Direct, uncompromising term for cruel harm and violation.

**Wickedness** - Timeless, weighty term that encompasses intentional evil and its harmful effects.

**Monstrosity** - Suggests something so wrong it's almost inhuman. Very strong condemnation.

Of these, **"abomination"** might be your best bet - it has the moral weight of "atrocity" but feels slightly fresher, and it naturally encompasses evil acts, violations of what's right, and the harm that results. It also has a certain literary quality that works well in serious writing.

**"Enormity"** is also excellent if you want something that many readers will find striking because they're not used to seeing it used correctly in its moral sense rather than just meaning "hugeness."

### Millard · 2025-08-16 20:10

I keep running into situations like, "all the explanations that contend that Mary is hiding something..." I want to eliminate one of the "that"-s./

### Claude · 2025-08-16 20:10

Here are several ways to eliminate one of those "that"s:

**Option 1: Drop the first "that"**
"All the explanations contending that Mary is hiding something..."

**Option 2: Drop the second "that"**
"All the explanations that contend Mary is hiding something..."

**Option 3: Use "which" for the first one**
"All the explanations which contend that Mary is hiding something..."

**Option 4: Restructure with "about"**
"All the explanations about Mary hiding something..."

**Option 5: Use a different verb structure**
"All the explanations claiming that Mary is hiding something..."
"All the explanations suggesting that Mary is hiding something..."

**Option 6: Flip the structure**
"All explanations contending that Mary is hiding something..."

I'd recommend **Option 1** ("contending that") as the most elegant solution. It eliminates the awkward double-"that" while maintaining the precise meaning and flow. The participle "contending" works smoothly here and is actually more concise than "that contend."

This pattern works for similar constructions: "theories arguing that," "claims suggesting that," "ideas proposing that," etc.
