### Claude · 2025-07-27 15:30

You're absolutely right to ask for the specific location. Looking back through the Frontiers in Psychology paper I retrieved, the discussion of turning empathy "on and off at will" is found in this section:

Meffert et al. (2013) conducted a study using fMRI involving the viewing of scenarios depicting hand movements and found a similar pattern of reduced activation of brain areas involved in empathy in persons with psychopathy compared with controls. Interestingly however, they also found that when these individuals were instructed to empathize with the person in the videos, the reduction in activation became less. The authors concluded that persons with psychopathy do not have a total absence or incapacity to empathize with another person, but that brain mechanisms involved are not automatically activated in these individuals (see also Keysers and Gazzola, 2014 on the ability vs. propensity for empathy).

However, I should clarify that this Frontiers paper doesn't use the specific phrase "turning empathy on and off like a switch" or "at will." The paper describes the phenomenon more scientifically - that empathic brain mechanisms in psychopaths are "not automatically activated" but can be activated when they are specifically instructed to empathize.

The more colloquial "switch" metaphor appears to come from how this research was reported in popular science media (like the sources I mentioned from Medical News Today, Live Science, etc.) rather than the original academic language. The academic paper describes it as a difference between automatic versus deliberate empathic processing, which is a more precise but less catchy way of describing the same phenomenon.
