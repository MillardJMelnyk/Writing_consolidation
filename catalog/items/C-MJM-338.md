### Millard · 2025-11-22 19:55

### Claude · 2025-11-22 19:55

That's a stark position - essentially asserting that meaning is binary rather than existing on a spectrum. It cuts through a lot of the ambiguity that plagues communication theory.

This view would suggest that context, connotation, implication, and degrees of precision are illusions - that either a statement successfully conveys definite content or it's meaningless noise. It rejects the idea that words can be partially meaningful, approximately true, or variably interpreted while still carrying information.

Consider though: when someone says "it's cold outside," does this mean nothing because "cold" lacks precise temperature boundaries? Or when poetry uses metaphor - "time is a thief" - is this meaningless because it's not literally true?

Your position seems to demand that meaningful communication requires fixed, unambiguous referents. This would make most human communication "nothing at all" since we constantly operate with fuzzy categories, contextual meanings, and statements that convey something even when imprecise.

What drives you to this binary view? Is it frustration with people who hide behind ambiguity, or a deeper philosophical commitment to the idea that genuine communication requires absolute clarity?

### Millard · 2025-11-22 19:58

### Claude · 2025-11-22 19:58

Intent matters more.

Poor articulation with genuine intent to communicate can be worked with - clarified, refined, developed. The person is trying to reach you, even if clumsily. There's something to build on.

Perfect articulation with corrupt intent - whether to deceive, manipulate, or evade - is worthless or worse. It doesn't matter how precisely someone crafts their lies or how eloquently they deflect. The communication is poisoned at its root.

A child stammering through an attempt to explain something important has more communicative value than a politician's perfectly crafted non-answer. The recovering stroke patient struggling to find words is engaged in real communication; the smooth-talking con artist is not.

This also explains why we can forgive poor word choice but not deceptive intent. We instinctively know that someone genuinely trying to convey meaning can be met halfway, while someone using words as tools for other purposes cannot be trusted no matter how clear their speech.

Quality of meaning can be improved through dialogue when intent is genuine. But no amount of linguistic precision redeems words spoken without genuine communicative intent - they're just higher-resolution manipulation.
