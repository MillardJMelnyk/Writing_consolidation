Premise is wrong. It's not "almost always mom."

**Overall perpetrator split:** varies by dataset, sometimes mother-majority, sometimes father-majority:

* UK sample (297 filicides, 45 filicide-suicides): 66% of perpetrators were fathers.
* Finland (200 filicides, 25-yr span): 59% by mothers, 39% by fathers, 2% by stepfathers.
* Filicide-suicide specifically: twice as many fathers as mothers committed filicide-suicide.

So base rates don't support "almost always mom" as a category-wide claim.

**Where the maternal skew is real:** the *altruistic/psychotic* motive subtype (kills to "save" the child from perceived suffering/evil, no rational motive) is disproportionately maternal. Maternal motives for filicide were predominantly altruistic (misguided love) or acutely psychotic (without rational motive). 85% of mothers vs 56% of fathers in this subtype had a diagnosis of major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, or other psychosis. Resnick found 67% of 88 filicidal mothers were psychotic; depression/psychosis more common in mothers than fathers overall.

**Mechanism:** postpartum/peripartum psychosis and severe depression are conditions specific to mothers biologically, producing delusional "protective" content (child possessed, doomed, suffering) that generates the "saving from evil" narrative. Fathers' filicides skew toward different motives — jealousy over the child preferring the mother, unemployment, lack of support — and different methods — non-accidental blunt injury more common with fathers, suffocation/smothering more common with mothers.

**Age-of-victim confound:** younger children are at greater risk from mothers, older children more often killed by fathers — mothers dominate the neonaticide/infanticide category where psychosis-driven cases cluster; that's likely inflating the "mom kills for delusional reasons" impression in your sample of news coverage.

**Bottom line:** your observation is an artifact of motive-subtype and media selection (psychotic/altruistic filicide narratives are more reportable/sensational), not the actual base-rate distribution of who kills kids. Cite the specific stud

### Claude · 2026-07-29 16:33

Because the underlying pathology is a specific perinatal event, not a general mental illness that both sexes get equally.

Postpartum psychosis is triggered by the abrupt post-delivery collapse of estrogen and progesterone (levels drop by orders of magnitude within 24-48 hours of birth), combined with sleep deprivation and, in many cases, an underlying bipolar diathesis that the hormonal crash unmasks. This crash doesn't happen to fathers because fathers don't gestate or give birth — there's no equivalent physiological event in male biology. That's the whole reason it's sex-specific: it's not "mothers are more prone to delusion," it's "childbirth itself is a psychiatric risk factor, and only people who give birth are exposed to it."

Rate: postpartum psychosis occurs in ~1-2 per 1000 births. Onset is typically within the first 2-4 weeks postpartum. It's strongly associated with bipolar disorder (up to 1 in 4-5 women with bipolar I experience a postpartum psychotic episode) and has a documented recurrence risk of ~30-50% in subsequent pregnancies for affected women.

So the female skew in "saving the child from evil" filicide isn't a behavioral or motivational difference between mothers and fathers. It's downstream of a hormonal/neuroendocrine event that is anatomically restricted to the sex that gestates and delivers.

### Claude · 2026-07-29 16:51

Likely reasons, stated directly:

1. **Base rate is tiny.** Filicide-suicide is rare (dozens of cases/year in a country the size of the US), and studies pull from single jurisdictions (one coroner's office, one country's national registry) because that's the only way to get case-file-level motive coding. n=30 is what you get from a "county coroner's office over X years." That makes multi-site standardization hard, so most papers stick to their own small sample rather than build on others'.
3. **Research funding/attention follows maternal filicide disproportionately** — EBSCO explicitly notes fathers are slightly more likely to kill their children overall, yet most literature attention focuses on maternal filicide. 4. **Ethical/access constraints.** Getting psychiatric case-file detail on a specific homicide requires forensic access most researchers don't have, which caps sample sizes hard regardless of interest.
