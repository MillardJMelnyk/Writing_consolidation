### Claude · 2026-08-16 17:50

It's a marketing angle, not a new discovery. Black seed oil is Nigella sativa — used for centuries. "Ethiopian" is a sourcing claim attached to a thymoquinone (TQ) potency story: Ethiopian-grown seed supposedly yields higher TQ than Turkish, Egyptian, or Indian. That's the whole pitch, amplified by TikTok since roughly last year.

The potency numbers are where it gets ugly. One seller advertises a batch above 5.6% TQ and claims that beats the premium competition by four or five times. A different Ethiopian-oil seller says the opposite outright: they warn against companies advertising high TQ, call 0.95% and "5X TQ" claims fraudulent, and say a lab test of such a product came back at 0.45%. Two vendors selling the same origin story, one calling the other's numbers fake. Almost nobody has the equipment to verify TQ, so the number on the label is unaudited.

Real TQ in cold-pressed Nigella oil generally lands well under 1%. Treat anything advertising multiples of that as unverified until a third-party COA says otherwise.

The evidence base: there's genuine research on Nigella sativa — mostly small trials, and mostly on blood pressure, lipids, and blood sugar, with modest effects. Nothing establishes that Ethiopian-origin oil outperforms other origins in a person. That comparison hasn't been tested; it's inferred from seed assays sellers commission themselves.

One thing that actually matters for you: black seed oil has documented interactions with blood-thinning and blood-sugar medication, and it can lower blood pressure. If you're chasing the mood-and-energy gap from the Klean question, this isn't the lever — there's no evidence it does anything there.

### Claude · 2026-08-16 17:51

On the blood pressure: black seed oil does lower BP modestly in trials, which cuts in your favor rather than against — the caution I gave applies to people already on antihypertensives, where the effects stack. Borderline and unmedicated, it's not a hazard. It's also not a treatment; the effect sizes are small, a few mmHg.

On parasite cleansing: that's the weak end of the claim set. Nigella sativa has genuine in-vitro and animal antiparasitic activity — it kills things in a dish. Human trials are thin, mostly small studies on giardia and schistosomiasis in endemic regions, not the general "everyone has parasites, cleanse quarterly" pitch that drives the TikTok version. That pitch is fiction. Healthy adults don't carry a parasite load that a cleanse clears, and the symptoms attributed to it — fatigue, brain fog, bloating — are the same symptoms every wellness product claims to fix.

The Mexico angle is the one real caveat. Actual parasitic infection is more plausible here than in the US, and if you have a genuine reason to suspect one — persistent GI symptoms, unexplained weight loss, recent bad water — the answer is a stool ova-and-parasite test at a lab, which is cheap here, followed by albendazole or whatever the result calls for. Those drugs work in days. Black seed oil is not a substitute and won't clear an established infection.

If you're circling this because of the mood-and-energy question: parasites are a long shot, and you'd almost certainly have GI symptoms alongside. The blood panel is the better first move, and it'd cover your BP workup too.
