### Claude · 2025-08-12 14:59

I don't think that's an accurate characterization of the consensus in political science or jurisprudence regarding the definition of law.

The relationship between content-independence and law is actually a matter of significant theoretical debate rather than overwhelming consensus. While some legal philosophers like Joseph Raz have argued that authority (including legal authority) must be content-independent to be genuine authority, this view is far from universally accepted.

Several important considerations complicate this claim:

1. **Competing theories of law**: Natural law theorists, legal realists, and others offer different criteria for what makes something law, not all of which require content-independence.
2. **Practical legal systems**: Many rules that are widely recognized as laws do have content-dependent elements - for example, laws that incorporate moral standards or require "reasonable" behavior.
3. **Constitutional principles**: Many legal systems include substantive constitutional principles that place content-based limits on what can count as valid law.
4. **Ongoing philosophical debate**: The question of whether law must be content-independent remains actively contested in legal philosophy, with sophisticated arguments on multiple sides.

Rather than there being an "overwhelming consensus," this appears to be an area where different theoretical approaches to understanding law continue to compete. The relationship between the formal properties of legal rules and their substantive content remains one of the central unresolved questions in jurisprudence.

### Millard · 2025-08-12 15:00

### Claude · 2025-08-12 15:00

The main criticisms of Raz's content-independence requirement are:

1. **Too narrow** - Excludes things we commonly consider laws (like constitutional principles or laws incorporating moral standards)
2. **Practical impossibility** - Real legal systems inevitably include content-dependent elements
3. **Conflates authority with law** - Just because authority should be content-independent doesn't mean law must be
4. **Ignores law's moral dimension** - Natural law theorists argue law necessarily connects to moral content
5. **Descriptively inaccurate** - Doesn't match how legal systems actually operate in practice
