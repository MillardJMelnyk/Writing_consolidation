### Millard · 2025-12-05 14:32

what's a "posthumanist thinker"?

### Claude · 2025-12-05 14:32

Posthumanism rejects the idea that "the human" is a fixed, universal category at the center of reality. Instead, it treats "human" as a contingent product—historically constructed, politically produced, and entangled with non-human forces.

Key moves: It denies human exceptionalism (we're not separate from or superior to nature/technology), questions human-centered frameworks for understanding knowledge and ethics, and emphasizes how agency is distributed across multiple actors—biological, technological, material, ecological.

In Akomolafe's case specifically, he emphasizes that "the human" is a creature of European colonialism, capitalism, and extractivism—not a timeless essence. His posthumanism involves decentering human concerns and attending to the agency of "the more-than-human" world (other species, systems, forces). It's tied to his critique of Western humanism as a tool of domination.

Contrasts with humanism, which assumes humans occupy a unique position with special rationality, rights, or moral standing that justifies our dominion over everything else.

### Claude · 2025-12-05 14:46

**None of them.**

The article explicitly states: "To my knowledge, no one is working anywhere in this space: the paradigmatic alternative to universal deprivation. No one is fleshing out the concept."

The closest candidates fail the test:

**Commons frameworks** (Bollier, Bauwels, Federici)—These relocate ownership from individual to collective, but the underlying principle of *universal deprivation* remains intact. It's reconfiguration, not eradication.

**Gift economy** (Eisenstein, Vaughan, Kimmerer)—The article's analysis explicitly rejects this: "You cannot share—in the typical sense of 'share'—what you don't already own...Gifting doesn't escape ownership—it presupposes it."

**Resource-based economy** (Joseph)—Still operates through allocation mechanisms that don't fundamentally escape deprive-first logic.

**Doughnut Economics** (Raworth)—Embedded within economic frameworks that presuppose ownership/scarcity management, not a paradigm shift away from ownership itself.

All these work within variations of the deprive-first structure—they reform or redistribute it, they don't eradicate it. They preserve the core principle: *some party controls access, depriving all others except at discretion.*

Share-first as you've defined it—universal access as default with deprivation only as exceptional case-by-case necessity—remains, per your analysis, an unoccupied conceptual space with no active theorists or designers working on it.

### Millard · 2025-12-05 14:47

What's the difference between major conceptions of "gift economy" and the share-first paradigm as the article describes?

### Claude · 2025-12-05 14:48

**Gift Economy:**

* I own X
* I choose to gift/share X with others as an act of generosity or reciprocity
* Access depends on my voluntary decision to redistribute what I own
* I retain the fundamental right to exclude; I'm choosing not to exercise it
* Deprive-first structure intact—I'm just being ethical about my exclusive power

**Share-First Paradigm:**

* No one owns X
* Everyone has default access to X
* Deprivation only when case-by-case necessity requires coordination
* Exclusion is structural impossibility, not a voluntary restraint
* Universal access first; restriction is exceptional and requires justification

The article's core critique: **Gift economy presupposes ownership.** You need to own something to gift it. The owner remains the gatekeeper—their choice to redistribute is admirable but discretionary.

Share-first doesn't add ethics to ownership; it eliminates ownership as a structural category entirely.

**Practical distinction:**

Gift economy (Kimmerer's *serviceberry* or Eisenstein's framework): "Those of us with resources should gift/share generously with the community out of gratitude and reciprocity."

* The owner decides when/how to share
* Generosity is the governing moral principle
* Access is contingent on owner benevolence

Share-first: "Everyone has access to the serviceberry tree. We coordinate *only if* there's insufficient fruit for everyone, or if someone is harvesting destructively."

* No one owns the tree
* Access is not contingent on anyone's choice
* Coordination is exception, not permission-granting

The article lumps gift economy with other ownership-reformulations (commons, communism) rather than true alternatives. All accept the ownership/deprive-first foundation; share-first rejects it entirely.
