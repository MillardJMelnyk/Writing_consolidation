**1. Steelman Debate**  
Discussion: When have you had to argue a position you disagreed with? Does defending an opposing view change your own?  
Exercises: Assign each student a position they disagree with; have them draft the 7-step structure; peer-review for fairness before rebuttal is written.

**2. Thesis Narrowing**  
Discussion: Why do vague claims resist evidence? Give an example of a claim that sounds strong but can't be tested.  
Exercises: Give students 5 broad claims ("Social media is bad for teenagers"); have them narrow each in 3 rounds, adding evidence requirements each round.

**3. Scope Control**  
Discussion: What's the difference between "Immigration lowers wages" and "Immigration lowers wages in low-skill sectors in the short term"?  
Exercises: Provide 10 universal claims; students rewrite each with explicit limits (time, place, population, context), then swap and check whether the limits are testable.

**4. Concession–Rebuttal Chains**  
Discussion: Why does conceding a point make an argument stronger, not weaker?  
Exercises: Give a one-sided paragraph; students insert a concession, then a rebuttal, using Although/Admittedly/Nevertheless/Even so; read pairs aloud and identify weak transitions.

**5. Weak Argument Surgery**  
Discussion: What's the difference between a weak argument and a wrong conclusion?  
Exercises: Provide 3-4 flawed opinion paragraphs; students annotate flaws (unsupported assumption, weak evidence, logical gap), then rewrite one in formal C2 prose.

**6. Counterargument Prediction**  
Discussion: Why answer objections you invented yourself rather than wait for someone to raise them?  
Exercises: Give a thesis statement; students list 3 objections ranked by strength, then write a one-sentence answer to each; class votes on which objection was hardest to answer.

**7. Certainty Calibration**  
Discussion: What changes in an argument when you replace "will" with "may"? Does hedging weaken or strengthen credibility?  
Exercises: Give 8 flat claims; students rewrite each at 3 certainty levels (may/likely/almost certainly); discuss which level fits the actual evidence given.

**8. Reverse Outline**  
Discussion: Does a strong editorial always follow the Thesis→Reason→Evidence→Counter→Rebuttal→Conclusion order? When would deviating help?  
Exercises: Give a real editorial; students map each paragraph to a slot in the structure; flag any missing or misordered element; rewrite the weakest transition.
