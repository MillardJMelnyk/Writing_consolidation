Ownership is a relationship between a party and a good. Strip away the legal overlays, the cultural variations, the philosophical justifications. At root, ownership implements a single principle:

---

**Part 2: Objections**

Every objection I've encountered falls into one of three categories: circular, misdirected, or premature.

**"Human nature is selfish."**

This assumes what it needs to prove. Humans raised under deprive-first conditions behave accordingly. That tells us nothing about what humans would do under share-first conditions. The argument smuggles the paradigm into the premise, then claims the paradigm is validated by the conclusion.

**"Tragedy of the commons."**

The "commons" in Hardin's formulation is a collectively-owned resource — a different configuration of deprivation, not the absence of it. The "tragedy" arises from competing private interests exploiting a shared pool. Under share-first, there are no private interests extracting from a pool. The scenario doesn't apply.

**"No one would produce anything."**

This assumes production is motivated solely by the prospect of exclusive possession. But people produce for use, for others, for meaning, for participation, for recognition. Deprive-first suppresses most of these motivations by making production contingent on ownership. The objection mistakes a symptom of the current paradigm for a fixed feature of human behavior.

**"It's just communism."**

Communism relocates ownership from individuals to the collective or the state. The excluding party changes; the structure doesn't. Communism is a reconfiguration of deprive-first, not a departure from it.

**"How would it actually work?"**

This demands implementation before the principle is even acknowledged. It's a deflection. If the principle is incoherent, say so and show why. If it's coherent but you doubt it can be implemented, that's a different conversation — and one that can't happen until the principle is engaged on its own terms.

**"Someone has to decide who gets what."**

Under scarcity, yes — allocation decisions must be made. But the question is: what is the default prior to the decision? Deprive-first says: no one has access until someone grants it. Share-first says: everyone has access until necessity requires restriction. The allocation problem exists under both paradigms. The question is which default it operates from.

**"People will take advantage."**

Some will. Under any system. The question is whether the structure itself generates and rewards exploitation, or whether it minimizes and isolates it. Deprive-first *systematizes* advantage-taking — it makes exclusive accumulation the path to security and power. Share-first removes that structural incentive.

---

**Part 3: Vision and Invitation**

What would we gain?

Not utopia. Not the elimination of conflict, scarcity, or difficult decisions. But a structural shift with consequences:

* **Security would not require accumulation.** Access to what you need would not depend on your ability to exclude others from it.
* **Cooperation would not require enforcement.** The default relationship between people would be mutual access, not mutual threat.
* **Power would lose its primary lever.** Control over others depends on controlling what they need. Remove deprivation as the default, and domination loses its structural foundation.
* **The question "who owns this?" would disappear.** In its place: "Is there a necessary reason to restrict access to this?" A harder question, but the right one.

This is not a blueprint. It is a direction.

The vigil has lasted long enough. I'm looking for people willing to turn their heads — not to look away from the problem, but to look *at* it, perhaps for the first time.

If you can see the inversion, and you're interested in honest, open, intelligent conversation about it — I want to hear from you.

**End draft.**
