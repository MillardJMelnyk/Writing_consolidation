### Claude · 2026-08-27 22:08

### Millard · 2026-08-27 22:10

What was the reason given for it at the time, and what is the reason for continuing it now?

### Claude · 2026-08-27 22:10

**1991 rationale:** the risk-based strategy started in 1988 — screen pregnant women, vaccinate only high-risk groups — wasn't working. Roughly a third to 40% of chronic infections had no identifiable risk factor, and a lot of childhood infections came from household contact rather than birth. Universal infant vaccination was adopted because targeting couldn't find the cases in time, and infants infected at birth become chronic carriers ~90% of the time versus ~5% for adults.

**Reason now:** mostly unchanged, plus a safety-net argument. Maternal HBsAg screening still fails — mislabeled results, no prenatal care, late-trimester infection, lab error — and the birth dose is what covers those misses. It also raises series completion rates, and immigration from high-prevalence regions keeps a reservoir in the population. CDC has actually tightened rather than loosened it: the 2018 guidance moved from "birth dose before discharge" to "within 24 hours" for medically stable infants over 2000g.

### Millard · 2026-08-27 22:12

What a shitty and I expect a deliberately obscure response. Speak plainly. High risk for what? Infected by what? Babies being infected with hepatitis b?

### Claude · 2026-08-27 22:12

Yes — babies getting hepatitis B.

"High risk" meant: the mother has hepatitis B. She passes it to the baby during delivery, through blood contact. That was the main worry.

The 1988 plan was: test every pregnant woman, and if she's positive, vaccinate her baby at birth. Sensible on paper.

It failed for two reasons:

1. **Testing missed mothers.** Results got lost, mislabeled, or never ordered. Women who showed up in labor with no prenatal care never got tested at all. So positive mothers slipped through and their babies got infected.
2. **Babies were getting infected who weren't born to infected mothers.** These were toddler-age infections picked up in the household — an infected father, grandmother, sibling. Hepatitis B survives on surfaces for days and spreads through minor blood contact: shared toothbrushes, razors, a cut, biting, scratches. Roughly a third of chronic cases had no traceable source at all.

So the argument was: we can't reliably identify which babies are at risk, and the consequence of missing one is severe — an infant who gets infected has about a 90% chance of becoming a lifelong carrier, versus about 5% for an adult who catches it. Lifelong carriers are the ones who get cirrhosis and liver cancer decades later.

The decision was to vaccinate all newborns because targeting the right ones didn't work, not because anyone thought newborns generally were engaged in risky behavior.

### Millard · 2026-08-27 22:13

### Claude · 2026-08-27 22:13

Small. Here are the actual numbers.

About 4 million births a year. Roughly 20,000 of those babies — 0.5% — were born to mothers who tested positive for hepatitis B. Without any vaccine or immune globulin, somewhere around 30–40% of those babies got infected, more like 70–90% if the mother was highly infectious. That works out to roughly 6,000–8,000 infected newborns a year, about 0.2% of all babies.

Then add the household transmission in the first few years of life. CDC's estimate for children under 10 acquiring hepatitis B was somewhere in the range of 16,000–30,000 a year, out of a population of ~35 million kids that age. Under 0.1% annually.

So no, it was never a large fraction of babies. The case rested on three things instead: the outcome for an infected infant is bad and permanent, the ones at risk couldn't be reliably identified in advance, and vaccinating everyone was cheap compared to the alternative.

Whether that tradeoff is the right one is a legitimate argument to have. But the honest framing is a small-probability, high-severity, low-cost-to-prevent calculation — not that babies in general were catching hepatitis B.

### Millard · 2026-08-27 22:16

And what's the treatment for hepatitis b if an infant contracts it?

### Claude · 2026-08-27 22:16

There is no cure. An infant who becomes a chronic carrier stays one.

What exists is management: monitoring liver enzymes and viral load for life, and antivirals (tenofovir, entecavir) started if the liver starts showing damage — usually decades later. Those suppress the virus, they don't clear it. Interferon is sometimes tried in children and clears it in a minority of cases.

The other half of the answer, and the one that matters here: if a baby is exposed at birth, hepatitis B immune globulin plus the vaccine within 12 hours prevents infection about 90–95% of the time. That's post-exposure, and it only works if you know the mother is positive.
