### Claude · 2026-09-07 01:43

Yes, structurally standard. This is a common product in Querétaro and the Bajío, and clause-for-clause about 85% of it matches what every firm in that market issues. There's no law regulating pólizas jurídicas or their cost, but they aren't illegal. That cuts both ways: no rules to protect you, and no rules being broken.

**Standard boilerplate — don't fight these:**  
Definitions section, landlord's 10-day notice duty, cessation when the landlord hires another lawyer or settles directly, no guaranteed timeline, eviction costs on the landlord, no liability for property damage, "not insurance" disclaimer, data-protection clause, jurisdiction and venue. The extinción de dominio clause (Décima tercera) reads harsh but has been standard in Mexican rental paperwork since the 2019 law. The exclusions list (Quinta) is almost entirely landlord-side risk.

**Non-standard or sloppy — worth calling out:**  
- The whole document says "Terreno." That's land, not a house. Coverage is "Desocupación legal del terreno." If they hand you this template for a house, the covered object is wrong in every clause.  
- Page 2 misspells "arrendamiento" as "arrentamiento" in their own boilerplate.  
- No signature line for you or the co-signer, yet Declaración IV binds you both to statements.  
- Cost blank.  
- I couldn't find Grupo Roselle anywhere online. Not evidence of anything — plenty of small despachos have no web presence — but ask for their RFC and verify the Zibatá address before money moves.

**Real concerns, ranked:**

**1. The pagarés (Anexo 6).** Industry-standard practice — firms in Querétaro advertise "generación de pagarés" as part of the package — but standard doesn't mean harmless. A pagaré is enforced in an expedited commercial suit where you carry the burden of disproving the debt. You have not seen how many, for what amounts, or what triggers their release. This is the single biggest thing in the file and it isn't even in this document.

**2. Your amendments void it.** Quinta: no coverage if the contract is modified without Grupo Roselle's knowledge. You're actively rewriting the landlord's contract. The póliza must be issued against your final amended text, or you've bought a dead product and the landlord has a grievance with your name on it.

**3. "Evento" fires on one missed month.** Also on unpaid utilities and on using the place for anything other than the stated purpose. If you're working from home, running a trading setup, teaching classes on camera — make sure "uso" in the base contract covers it. That clause is how a landlord who wants you out manufactures a trigger.

**4. Obligado solidario.** Jointly and severally liable, gets registered in the bureau alongside you, and signs the same declarations. Most Querétaro firms want a co-signer who owns property in the state. That's a large favor to ask of someone.

**5. The bureau.** Buró de Rentas is a real registry that landlords and agents feed tenant records into — a private database with no regulator behind it. Your ARCO rights on paper are only as good as the operator.

**6. Nothing here says you pay for it.** Read the carátula: cost is listed with no party assigned. No clause obligates the tenant to fund the póliza. If you're being asked to pay, that obligation lives in the base contract — find it and negotiate it there. Market rate runs from a few thousand pesos up, generally priced off the monthly rent.

**Should you be worried?** Not about this document — it's a service contract between the landlord and a law firm, and you're barely a party to it. Be worried about the three anexos it references (3, 4 and 6) that you haven't read, and about the fact that your negotiated amendments and this póliza are on a collision course. Get the base contract and all anexos in hand as one package before you sign any of it.

Yes, ask. But know what you're asking for and what you'll realistically get.

Is it standard?
---------------

The clause comes from a template that circulates widely in Mexico. The model version makes the tenant exclusively responsible for any activity, direct or indirect, by the tenant, "de sus parientes, empleados o cualquier persona que directa o indirectamente ocupe o llegue a ingresar al inmueble" (their relatives, employees, or any person who directly or indirectly occupies or enters the property).

So the "anyone you let in" scope is standard. Both your contracts copy it. Every contract you're offered will have some version.

**Why landlords use it.** The law gives the owner a good-faith defense — they must show they acted with due diligence when renting and didn't know about the illegal activity. The clause exists to build that evidence trail. That's a legitimate purpose, and it's worth understanding because it tells you which parts you can negotiate.

**Here's the key point:** the landlord's good-faith defense is served by your declarations, the residential-use restriction, and her having checked your ID. None of that requires you to pay for the house. The payment obligation is bolted on top and does nothing for her legal defense.

Not everything in ACCR's version is standard. The common template makes the tenant "responsible" and requires them to notify authorities. It doesn't always include an express duty to pay the property's sale value. Your Fortezza lease does have that duty, so you've seen two out of two — but it's the harsh end of the range, not the floor.

Should you ask?
---------------

Yes. Three reasons.

**It costs nothing.** Worst case they say no and you're where you started.

**Part of it is legally weak.** Under Mexican civil law a penalty clause can't exceed the value of the main obligation — here, the rent. An automatic obligation to pay a house is challengeable on that basis. If they instead call it compensation for damages, they'd have to prove you actually caused the loss. Either way the clause is softer in court than it looks on paper. Ask your lawyer to confirm the article in the Querétaro code before you cite it.

**The rescission trigger is the part worth fixing most.** Forfeiture of the house is a remote scenario. Losing the lease because your address appeared in someone's case file is not. "Relacionado" (connected to) is the word doing the damage.

What to ask for
---------------

Don't ask them to delete it. Ask for three narrowings:

**1. Inside the property only.** Remove "dentro o fuera del inmueble" (inside or outside the property). Crimes committed elsewhere shouldn't touch your lease.

**2. Proven, not suspected.** Replace "sospecha o comprobación" (suspicion or proof) with a requirement of formal proceedings against the property, and make the payment obligation apply only on a final judgment declaring forfeiture — not on a case merely being opened.

**3. Your fault required.** Limit liability to acts you committed or that you knew about and failed to prevent. Remove strict liability for a guest's conduct.

Here's text you can propose:

> Se modifica el párrafo final de la Cláusula Décimo Sexta para quedar como sigue: la rescisión procederá únicamente cuando exista un procedimiento formal iniciado por autoridad competente en contra del inmueble, derivado de hechos ilícitos cometidos **dentro** del inmueble por "EL ARRENDATARIO" o por personas a quienes éste haya autorizado el acceso, y siempre que dichos hechos sean atribuibles a "EL ARRENDATARIO" por dolo o culpa. La obligación de resarcimiento prevista en esta cláusula procederá únicamente en caso de que se dicte sentencia firme que declare la extinción de dominio del inmueble.

(Rescission applies only where a competent authority has formally opened proceedings against the property, arising from unlawful acts committed **inside** the property by the tenant or persons the tenant authorized to enter, and only where those acts are attributable to the tenant through intent or negligence. The compensation obligation applies only if a final judgment declaring forfeiture is issued.)

Same edit needs applying to Clause Fourth and Clause Fifteenth, which repeat the same exposure.

They'll likely accept **1** and **3** — those are reasonable and don't weaken the landlord's real protection. Point 2 is the one they'll resist, because "sospecha" is deliberately broad.
