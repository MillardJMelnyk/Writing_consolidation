### Claude · 2025-06-28 20:12

Before Gandhi's rise to prominence, there were several revolutionary groups and individuals in India who advocated for and practiced violent resistance against British rule. These revolutionaries made the British colonial authorities increasingly concerned about armed uprising, which indeed made Gandhi's philosophy of non-violent resistance appear as a more manageable alternative.

Key violent revolutionary movements and figures included:

**Bengal Revolutionary Movement**: This was perhaps the most significant early revolutionary movement, active from the early 1900s through the 1930s. Groups like Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar were involved in bombings, assassinations, and robberies to fund their activities. Notable figures included Khudiram Bose, who was executed at age 18 for attempting to assassinate a British judge, and Bagha Jatin, who led armed resistance.

**Hindustan Republican Association (HRA)**: Founded in 1924, later renamed Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA), this group was responsible for several high-profile attacks. Members included Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru, who were executed for killing a British police officer and later throwing bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly.

**Revolutionary activities in Maharashtra and Punjab**: Groups in these regions carried out assassinations of British officials and collaborators. The Chapkar brothers in Maharashtra were early examples, assassinating British officials in the 1890s.

**Chittagong Armoury Raid**: Led by Surya Sen in 1930, revolutionaries temporarily seized control of Chittagong and declared independence.

These violent revolutionary activities, bombings, and assassinations created a climate of fear among British officials and made them increasingly worried about widespread armed rebellion. The British were particularly concerned after events like the 1857 rebellion and were anxious to avoid another large-scale violent uprising.

In this context, Gandhi's philosophy of satyagraha (non-violent resistance) offered the British a way to engage with Indian independence demands without facing the constant threat of bombs and assassinations. While Gandhi was still challenging British rule, his methods were predictable and manageable compared to underground revolutionary violence.

This dynamic gave Gandhi additional leverage in negotiations, as British officials recognized that suppressing his movement too harshly might drive more Indians toward the violent revolutionary path.

For many Indians, especially younger generations, these revolutionaries represent a more direct, uncompromising form of patriotism that resonates strongly with their sense of national pride.
