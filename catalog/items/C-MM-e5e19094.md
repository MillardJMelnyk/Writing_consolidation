### Claude · 2026-10-01 17:20

There's no peer-reviewed study on this specific behavior; the explanation you'll find is behaviorists' and vets' consensus, layered on a few adjacent studies about dog–human contact.

**Adjacent research (measured, not about the paw itself):** Studies show friendly physical contact with a dog can raise oxytocin and heart rate variability in both partners while lowering human cortisol. Researchers at the University of Jyväskylä found that during social tasks like stroking and playing, dog and owner physiology co-modulate; that work was published in Scientific Reports.

**Professional consensus on a relaxed, resting paw (opinion, not tested):**  
- Contact and bonding, Highly likely the main driver: a relaxed dog that settles its paw on your arm, muscles soft and breathing slow, is probably showing trust and affection.  
- Puppy origin, Likely: newborn puppies rhythmically knead their mother's belly with their forelegs while nursing, and adult pawing is commonly traced to that.  
- Learned reinforcement, Likely as a contributor: a paw on someone is usually seen as endearing and results in petting, so the dog learns the act gets attention and keeps doing it.  
- Dominance, Highly unlikely: some people believe it signals dominance, but current trainers don't support that reading.

**When it means something else:** pawing can signal stress, which shows up as flat ears or lip-smacking, and a dog that wants engagement usually pairs the paw with a steady gaze, soft whine, wagging tail, or play bow. A limp paw on a chilled-out dog is neither.
