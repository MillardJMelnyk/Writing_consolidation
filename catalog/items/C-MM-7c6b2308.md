### Millard · 2026-09-04 13:30

Is the size disparity between the control and active arm groups typical

### Claude · 2026-09-04 13:31

Yes. 2:1 is standard for placebo-controlled trials in infants — it gets more children on the candidate drug and fewer on placebo, which parents accept more readily, and it costs surprisingly little statistical power (about 6% versus 1:1 for the same total enrollment).

The tradeoff is exactly what you saw: the control arm gets small, so rare-event counts there are noisy and comparisons on deaths or anaphylaxis carry wide uncertainty. That's why FDA flagged the death imbalance as needing case-by-case review rather than resolving it statistically — 4 events in 1,797 can't be compared cleanly to 12 in 3,710.

### Claude · 2026-09-04 13:32

What happened to the death imbalance after FDA raised it
========================================================

**The advisory committee vote, June 8, 2023.** FDA put the imbalance on the agenda as one of two named safety issues. After a full-day meeting, the committee voted 21–0 that the benefit-risk profile was favorable for first-season infants, and 19–2 for second-season high-risk children. The two dissents were on the second-season indication, not the deaths.

**FDA's own reasoning.** From the briefing document: causes scattered across cardiac, infectious, malignant and accidental categories with no organ-system clustering; control-arm causes looked the same as nirsevimab-arm causes; the treated-arm rate of 3.1 per 1,000 sat far below the 28 per 1,000 global infant mortality baseline; and a dose-response check found 0.3% mortality at both 50 mg and 100 mg, with zero deaths at 200 mg. No dose-response is what you'd want to see if the drug were killing infants. Approval followed in July 2023, then ACIP recommendation in August.

**Post-marketing, US.** CDC's Vaccine Safety Datalink ran a self-controlled study across two seasons, over 74,000 exposed infants. Seizures, ITP, drug reaction, fever/sepsis — no elevated risk in any. Zero anaphylaxis cases. Eighteen non-anaphylactic allergic reactions, nearly all urticaria, mostly same-day. The one thing that flickered was seizures: RR 3.50 and 4.38 in the two age strata, both non-significant (4 and 5 cases), and the prior season showed nothing. Mortality was not a monitored outcome — self-controlled designs can't study death, since the person has to survive to contribute a control window.

**Post-marketing, Spain.** First national campaign, 23.1 serious-event reports per 100,000 doses, two fatalities among 67 reported cases. No new signals, no ADE.

**Population-level.** US infant mortality fell from 5.63 to 5.49 per 1,000 in 2024, the first full season of wide availability, alongside a 28–43% drop in infant RSV hospitalizations.

**The live dissent.** A 2024 paper in *Current Issues in Molecular Biology* argues the ADE question was never properly closed — nirsevimab's Fc effector functions weren't fully characterized preclinically, and the authors point to a French neonatal mortality analysis and the absence of an all-cause hospitalization reduction as unexplained. **What was never done.** No trial was powered for mortality. No post-marketing study has directly tested it. The imbalance was resolved by case-by-case adjudication and background-rate comparison, and it will stay resolved that way unless someone runs a cohort study with death as the endpoint.

### Millard · 2026-09-04 13:36

What's the relationship between RSV and pneumonia

### Claude · 2026-09-04 13:36

RSV is one of the viruses that causes pneumonia. It infects the upper airway first, and in some infants spreads down into the small airways and air sacs — that's bronchiolitis or pneumonia depending on where it lands.

It also opens the door to bacterial pneumonia by damaging the airway lining, so a kid can get RSV first and a bacterial infection on top of it.

### Millard · 2026-09-04 13:37

When did RSV first begin to be diagnosed

### Claude · 2026-09-04 13:38

1955 — isolated from chimpanzees at Walter Reed, called "chimpanzee coryza agent." Chanock found the same virus in Baltimore infants in 1957 and renamed it for the fused cell clumps (syncytia) it makes in culture.

Routine clinical testing came much later: immunofluorescence in the 1970s, rapid antigen tests in the 1980s, PCR from the 1990s on. So the deaths were happening all along, just filed as bronchiolitis or pneumonia of unknown cause.
