### Claude · 2026-08-29 19:58

It's a real lawsuit dressed up as news it isn't.

**What actually happened.** On Jan. 21, 2026, Children's Health Defense filed a civil suit in the U.S. District Court for D.C. accusing the AAP of a decades-long scheme to mislead people about the childhood vaccine schedule, pleading it as a RICO violation. Filed by attorney Richard Jaffe for CHD, two physicians, and several parents; docket 1:26-cv-00171. The post is dated Aug 9, 2026 — seven months after the filing — and is written as "BREAKING." That alone tells you what the account is doing.

**The framing is the tell.** "SLAMMED with a federal RICO lawsuit" describes one private advocacy group paying a filing fee. No prosecutor, no agency, no grand jury. Filing a complaint requires zero evidence, only allegations. Nothing has been proven, no court has found anything, and the words in the graphic ("EVIDENCE," "FEDERAL RICO LAWSUIT") are doing the work that a verdict would normally do. The AAP moved to dismiss on April 3 on several grounds including standing, with plaintiffs' response due June 9. As of now I found no ruling — that's a genuine gap, not a hedge.

**Odds, honestly.** Low. Civil RICO is the most over-pled statute in federal court and judges kill it routinely. The specific hurdles here: standing and proximate cause (the AAP doesn't vaccinate anyone — doctors do, on a CDC schedule, so causation runs through multiple independent actors), Rule 9(b) particularity on each mail/wire fraud predicate, a four-year limitations period against a "decades-long" theory, and the First Amendment — publishing medical recommendations is speech, and calling speech a racketeering predicate is exactly what courts refuse to do. CHD has run this play before: in CHD v. Meta the district court rejected the RICO claim for failure to establish a wire fraud predicate, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed dismissal.

**Where the underlying grievance is not nonsense.** Two things in the complaint are substantively defensible independent of the fraud theory: there is no randomized trial of the cumulative schedule as administered, and vaccination-rate financial incentives to pediatricians plus manufacturer funding of the AAP are documented facts, not inventions. That's a real conflict-of-interest argument. The move from "conflicted and under-studied" to "criminal racketeering enterprise" is a leap the complaint asserts rather than demonstrates, and it's the leap the court will actually rule on.

**What the suit is really for.** Discovery. The prize is subpoenaed internal AAP and sponsor documents — the tobacco playbook they keep invoking. Which is also why a 12(b)(6) dismissal ends it, since discovery doesn't start until the complaint survives.

**On the source.** Nicolas Hulscher writes for the McCullough Foundation's Substack; the account reposting it has fenbendazole/ivermectin protocols in its avatar. That's advocacy media with a supplement-adjacent audience, not reporting. Doesn't make the filing fake — it does mean every framing choice in that post is optimized for outrage, and none of the legal weaknesses above will ever appear in their coverage.

### Claude · 2026-08-29 20:01

Poor on the merits, and that's not the point of the litigation.

**The losses, which are most of it:**

* **CHD v. FDA** (EUA of COVID shots for kids, filed Jan. 2022) — dismissed for lack of standing in W.D. Texas, with prejudice; the court held neither the parents nor CHD showed an injury in fact, and added that the claims would fail on the merits anyway. Fifth Circuit affirmed on standing; CHD petitioned the Supreme Court.
* **CHD v. Facebook/Meta/Poynter/Science Feedback** — dismissed by Judge Illston in 2021 (no state action, no Lanham Act claim, and the RICO claim failed for want of a wire-fraud predicate); Ninth Circuit affirmed in 2024.
* **CHD v. Rutgers** — district court dismissed everything as moot or failing to state a claim; the Third Circuit found Rutgers didn't violate students' rights, though it held the mootness dismissal was error as to the damages period.
* **Doe v. Zucker / NY healthcare-worker federal track** — the Second Circuit vacated the preliminary injunction; that suit was voluntarily dismissed and refiled in state court.
* **LAUSD employee mandate** (allied, not CHD-filed) — Ninth Circuit upheld dismissal in July 2025, Supreme Court denied cert in 2026.
* **CHD's motion to intervene** in the AAP's suit against HHS/RFK Jr. — denied Feb. 27, 2026, in a one-sentence order; CHD is appealing.

**The wins, which are narrow and mostly state-court or procedural:**

* **Medical Professionals for Informed Consent v. Bassett** (NY) — Judge Neri struck the state healthcare-worker COVID mandate as void in Jan. 2023, on the ground the health department lacked legislative authority. The Fourth Department dismissed the state's appeal as moot in Oct. 2023 without overturning it, so it stands but is a trial-level state ruling on agency authority, not on vaccine safety.
* **CHD-CA v. Piedmont USD** — district backed down and settled after an adverse preliminary ruling.
* **V.D. v. New York** — preliminary injunction denied, voluntarily dismissed, but NYSED corrected its guidance to districts along the way.

**The pattern.** They almost never lose on "are vaccines safe." They lose on standing, state action, mootness, and failure to plead a predicate act — the doorway questions. When they win, it's on administrative-authority grounds (an agency exceeded what the legislature gave it), in state court, on a mandate rather than on the science. No court has ever adjudicated their central factual claim, because no case has survived long enough to reach discovery on it.

**Which is exactly why the AAP suit matters to read carefully.** The one prior time CHD pleaded civil RICO, a federal judge threw out the predicate and the Ninth Circuit agreed. They're running the same statute again against a bigger target with better-sympathized plaintiffs (parents of dead children, delicensed physicians), which improves the standing story considerably over the FDA case. It doesn't fix the proximate-cause or First Amendment problems.

One legal scholar quoted in the NPR-affiliate coverage, Dorit Reiss, made the point directly: litigation gets CHD attention even when courts rule against it. Judged as a fundraising and narrative engine rather than as a law practice, the record looks a lot better than it does in the reporters.
