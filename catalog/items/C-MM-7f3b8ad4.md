Facebook groups for crowd-sourced opinions and lawyer referrals in English: Queretaro Expats (facebook.com/groups/queretaroexpats), International Newcomers and Expats of Querétaro (facebook.com/groups/NewcomersClubQueretaro), Foreigners in Queretaro (facebook.com/groups/foreignersinqueretaro). No. An orientation tells you what the law says; it won't tell you whether your case is worth filing, because that turns on facts they won't dig into in a 20-minute walk-in — what your contract says, what you can prove, how much money is actually at stake, and whether the landlord has anything to collect from.

For "is it worth it," the Centro de Justicia Alternativa is the better first move anyway. Mediation is free, it's fast, and the landlord's response to a mediation invitation tells you most of what you need: if he shows up and negotiates, you settle without a lawyer; if he ignores it, you know litigation is the only path and you can price it out from there.

Rough shape of the math in Querétaro: a civil arrendamiento suit means a private lawyer, usually a retainer plus a percentage, and months of hearings. If the disputed amount is one or two months' rent, the lawyer costs more than the claim. If it's a deposit plus damages running into serious money, or you're facing eviction and need time, it changes.

### Claude · 2026-09-02 00:59

A missing copy doesn't kill you. A lease is provable by other evidence in a Mexican civil court, and possession plus payment is usually enough to establish that a tenancy exists.

What to do, in order:

Ask the landlord or the agency for a copy. Do it in writing — WhatsApp, email — because if he refuses, that refusal is itself useful later. If a real estate agent or a póliza jurídica company brokered the rental, they hold a file copy and will usually hand it over without argument.

Check whether anyone else signed. A fiador or aval got a copy. So did any co-tenant.

Meanwhile, build the paper trail that proves the terms even without the document: bank transfers or deposit slips showing rent amount and dates, the deposit payment, utility bills in your name at that address, WhatsApp threads where the terms come up, the original listing, your INE/passport-and-address paperwork.

What you lose without the contract is the specific clauses — notice period, deposit conditions, penalties. What you keep is the fact of the tenancy, the rent amount, and the deposit. If the dispute is about a clause, the missing copy hurts. If it's about the deposit or about him not fixing something, it barely matters, because the Código Civil de Querétaro sets those default obligations whether or not the contract restates them.

One thing works in your favor: if the landlord won't produce the contract either, the gaps get filled by the civil code defaults rather than by whatever he claims it said.

### Claude · 2026-09-02 01:06

Honest answer: the evidence-gathering is realistic and cheap, so do it regardless. The enforcement side is weaker than my last message implied.

What actually holds up in Mexico:

Photos and a message trail work. Mexican civil courts and mediators take documentary evidence seriously, and a landlord with no before-photos and no invoice against a tenant with both is in a bad position. That part is real.

Mediation at the CJA is real and free, but it's voluntary. She can simply not show up, and there's no penalty. In practice, landlords who know they're on thin ice often do show, because a convenio signed there is enforceable and cheaper than court for them too. But you can't compel her.

Here's the part that matters: deposit recovery in Mexico is notoriously hard to enforce. Landlords keeping deposits on thin pretexts is common, tenants rarely sue, and landlords know it. If she just keeps the money, your only real remedy is a civil suit, and for a one-or-two-month deposit the lawyer costs more than the deposit. That's the arithmetic she's counting on.

So the realistic leverage isn't legal, it's practical:

If you're still living there and still owe rent, the last month's rent is your actual leverage. Many tenants in Mexico simply live out the deposit and don't pay the final month. It's technically a breach of contract if the contract forbids it — but she'd have to sue you to recover it, and the same arithmetic that protects her protects you. That's the honest picture, not a recommendation; it depends on your contract and whether you want a clean exit reference.

If you've already moved out and she has the money, your leverage is close to zero beyond a mediation invitation and the nuisance of it.

### Claude · 2026-09-02 01:07

Legally, no. Practically, often yes.

The law is clear: a deposit is security, not her money. Under the Querétaro civil code she can only deduct for unpaid rent, unpaid utilities, or damage beyond normal wear and tear, and she has to be able to justify the deduction. Ordinary deterioration from living there is explicitly not chargeable to you. A worn toilet part is her cost.

But there's no agency that enforces this. No inspector, no penalty, no automatic process. If she keeps it, nothing happens to her unless you personally take action. That's the gap she's operating in, and she knows it — it's the single most common landlord-tenant complaint in Mexico.

Your enforcement options, honestly ranked:

Mediation at the CJA. Free, but she can decline to attend.

Civil suit. You'd win with decent evidence, but it takes months and a private lawyer. For a deposit under roughly 30–40 thousand pesos, the cost exceeds the recovery. Above that it starts making sense.

Pressure without law. A clear, documented written demand citing the civil code, sent before she's dug in, moves a surprising number of landlords who don't want the hassle and don't know whether you'll actually file.

So the real answer is that whether she keeps it depends less on the law than on whether she thinks you'll be a nuisance. Being obviously organized — photos, dated messages, a written demand that sounds like it came from someone who's talked to a lawyer — is worth more than the law itself here.

How much is the deposit, and have you moved out yet? Those two answers change the recommendation completely.
