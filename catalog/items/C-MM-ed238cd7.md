### Claude · 2026-09-09 21:21

Yes — three that still stand.

**Deaths aren't cases.** They measured mortality only. Vaccines mainly cut infections, and for measles and pertussis the case-fatality rate had already collapsed for other reasons, so a vaccine that stops nearly all cases barely moves a death-rate curve. The McKinlays themselves later called the anti-vaccine reading of their paper an egregious misinterpretation, saying effective vaccines clearly matter for containing a disease once its prevalence is down.

**Their foundation got knocked out.** They had no alternative explanation of their own and pointed to McKeown. Szreter's 1988 critique found McKeown had misread the death records, confusing tuberculosis with bronchitis and pneumonia, and that "rising standards of living" lumped together unrelated things; in the reinterpretation, clean water and milk supplies mattered more, and the social conditions McKeown credited were partly harmful. Colgrove's 2002 review states McKeown's conclusions have been largely discredited by subsequent research. Not debunked since.

**The data stop in 1973, right before medicine started paying.** Ford et al. 2007 attributed roughly 47% of the 1980–2000 US coronary heart disease mortality drop to medical and surgical treatments, versus about 44% to risk-factor changes. That doesn't touch their 1900–1973 numbers, but it kills any attempt to extend the conclusion to the present.

Nobody has overturned the 3.5% figure itself for the period and diseases they covered.

### Claude · 2026-09-09 21:29

Your premise is wrong. Reducing incidence *is* the pro-vaccine argument. Death reduction isn't a separate claim, it's arithmetic downstream of it: deaths = cases × case-fatality. Take out 99% of cases and you take out 99% of deaths even if case-fatality never budges. Nobody claims a vaccine makes an infected person more likely to survive — that's what treatment claims to do, not prophylaxis.

Which is why M&M's method can't test the claim. Their yardstick is share of the total mortality decline since 1900, and that total was 12.14 deaths per 1,000. By the early 1960s measles was killing a few hundred Americans a year. Wipe out every one of those deaths and you move that yardstick by a rounding error. Their result is fully compatible with the measles vaccine having eliminated 100% of remaining measles deaths. The instrument can't see it. That's a resolution limit, not a null finding.

So M&M is the right paper for "did medicine drive the 20th-century mortality collapse" — no, it didn't — and the wrong paper for "does this vaccine prevent deaths." For that you need disease-specific counts before and after, or trials with death endpoints. Those exist and are the actual evidence base, not the aggregate curve.

On the CDC: what happened is real but it's not what you're treating it as. In November 2025 the CDC page was rewritten under RFK Jr.'s HHS to say the claim "vaccines do not cause autism" is not evidence-based because studies haven't ruled out the possibility. It's a wording change on a webpage, not new data, and no study was retracted. And it's a different logical animal from what you're arguing — that's a can't-prove-a-negative claim about harm. Efficacy is a positive claim, directly measurable, and it's been measured. The autism edit doesn't transfer.

Not a prediction — they wrote it as already happening, in the paper's opening section.
