### Claude · 2026-06-11 00:10

That's a fascinating and very specific symptom. The "exactly the same spot" detail rules out almost everything people normally blame for buffering.

**What this almost certainly is: a corrupted or incomplete file on the CDN edge server.**

Your internet connection is irrelevant here — the problem is upstream of you. Here's the logic:

* If it were *your* bandwidth or network, the sticking point would be random or load-dependent, not deterministic.
* If it were a general server load problem, other users would hit it at random times, not at the same timestamp.
* The fact that the *same moment in the content* always fails means the content file itself has a problem at that byte offset — a corrupted chunk, a missing segment, or a bad index entry in the stream manifest.

**What's actually happening technically:**

Streaming services (Netflix, YouTube, etc.) use segmented streaming (HLS or DASH). The video is sliced into 2–10 second chunks. One specific chunk at that timestamp is either corrupted on the edge node serving you, has a malformed header, or the manifest points to a segment that doesn't exist or times out consistently.

**What to try:**

1. **Force a different CDN edge node.** Use a VPN, switch to a different network (mobile hotspot), or even just change your DNS to 1.1.1.1 or 8.8.8.8. Any of these can cause the service to route you to a different server that has a clean copy.
2. **Clear the app's cache completely** — not just close it. On a smart TV or streaming device this sometimes holds a bad cached manifest.
3. **Report it to the service** with the exact title, episode, and timestamp. CDN corruption is something only they can fix by invalidating and re-fetching that edge copy. Most services have a way to flag playback issues.
4. **Try a different device or app** (browser vs. native app, for example). Different apps may hit different CDN endpoints or use different chunk sizes, landing on a clean copy.

Your speeds are genuinely irrelevant to this problem. A 10 Mbps connection would play the same content fine from a clean edge node.

What is a CDN edge node

### Claude · 2026-06-11 00:11

**CDN = Content Delivery Network.**

Instead of Netflix (for example) having one giant server somewhere that streams to everyone on earth, they have hundreds of servers scattered around the world — in major cities, in ISP data centers, sometimes literally inside your internet provider's building. Those local servers are **edge nodes**.

**How it works:**

When you press play, the service doesn't route your request all the way back to Netflix headquarters. It routes you to the nearest/fastest edge node, which already has a cached copy of that content. You're pulling the video from maybe 20 miles away instead of 2,000 miles away — which is why latency is low and speeds are fast.

**The corruption problem:**

That edge node got its copy of the content by downloading it from the origin server at some point. If something went wrong during *that* transfer — a blip, a partial write, a bad segment — the edge node has a damaged copy. It serves that damaged copy to everyone it routes. You're not the only one hitting that wall; anyone else the CDN is routing to that same node hits the same sticking point in that same piece of content.

**Simple analogy:**

The origin server is the master printing press. Edge nodes are local warehouses with pre-printed copies. If one warehouse got a damaged shipment of page 47, everyone who checks out a book from that warehouse finds page 47 torn. The printing press is fine. Other warehouses are fine. Just that one local copy.

That's why routing yourself differently (VPN, hotspot) often fixes it instantly — you just get assigned a different warehouse.

### Millard · 2026-06-11 00:19

Is there any chance there's a problem with my graphics card?

### Claude · 2026-06-11 00:19

No. A GPU problem would never produce that symptom.

If your graphics card were struggling, you'd see: dropped frames, visual artifacts (blocky garbage, flickering, wrong colors), stuttering that varies with scene complexity, or a crash/freeze of the whole system. Those symptoms would be inconsistent and tied to rendering load.

A stream stopping at an exact timestamp and being skippable past that point means the **data** never arrived — the player got nothing to decode at that moment. A GPU problem is downstream of data delivery; it can't cause the data to be missing.
