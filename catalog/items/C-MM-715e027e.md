### Claude · 2026-06-10 16:24

Short version first: morality is a code, ethics is an inquiry.

**Morality** tells you what to do and not do — it's prescriptive, usually socially enforced, and its authority tends to be external (God, law, community consensus, tradition). It operates by labeling: this is right, that is wrong. Compliance is the goal. It's essentially a control system.

**Ethics** asks *why* — it's investigative, concerned with the conditions that produce good or harmful outcomes, and its authority is internal (reason, honest observation, genuine care). It doesn't hand you a verdict, it hands you a framework for thinking. Development is the goal.

The confusion is that people use the words interchangeably, which flattens the distinction. But it matters: you can be highly moral — perfectly compliant — and completely unethical in any meaningful sense. And you can violate a moral code for genuinely ethical reasons.

Your framework actually maps onto this cleanly. What you described — letting frustration be data, understanding its source, developing naturally toward less destructive expression — that's ethical reasoning. It's not asking "is cussing wrong?" It's asking "what is this signal, where does it come from, what would genuine resolution look like?" Morality would just say don't do it. Which, as you noted, corrupts the data.

The book title is essentially this argument in four words.

### Claude · 2026-06-10 16:35

That's a genuine bind, not just a stylistic problem. Wittgenstein spent a career on exactly this wall — the limits of language are the limits of what can be said, and the thing you're trying to say is partly *about* those limits.
