Perceived limitations, whether they originate in honest, careful thinking or paranoid terror, *cannot* be true. The question whether they are true is ridiculous as long as we refuse to move out of the hypothetical and into the factual because, by definition, the hypothetical only has a place in the *absence of fact.*

What’s more, there is only one way to obtain facts: *doing* something. Honestly exposing ourselves to and interacting with what’s really going on.

That means precisely: moving *out* of the hypothetical and *into* the actual. We have no facts other than from experience.

This is obvious and easily demonstrable. As soon we allow facts into our mental mix, hypotheticals such as the occult, beliefs, superstitions, delusions, etc., *automatically lose their power.* You can see this happen with a person in real time. It’s unmistakable. You can watch their mind shift into a completely different, far saner and calmer mode. It’s not hard at all to induce this, except for one thing:

*To the degree that a person clings to their infantilism, they will fight against accepting certain facts into their mental mix, even if only to admit them as considerable, let alone consider them honestly.*

The reason for this has nothing at all to do with what’s “really real” or factual or intelligent or advisable. It concerns just one, simple, far more basic thing: they feel, however consciously or not, the mortal threat waiting to accost them were they to merely admit: not that certain information *is* factual, but only that it *could be factual*. The door to investigating that angle must remain blocked by pretending there’s nothing to investigate. The results of investigating are not the imminent danger. The openness and honesty involved in real investigation are the killers of their blind fidelity to the *narrative* they’re clinging to for sanity and dear life: the narrative for which, undoubtedly, they have already sacrificed much in order to stay clung.

It’s no wonder that all the most important areas of human life—personal relationships, business, government, and especially those infernal bedfellows religion and the military—revolve around the core of “sacrifice” and are permeated with its theme.

Such people are gruesomely enslaved. We call them “cultists” when they seem wildly strange and different from ourselves, pretending that our own particular enslavement to our own particular *survival/salvation narratives* is virtuous, while theirs is vile.

There’s a simple, foolproof litmus test for cultism. It tells us where a person’s rationality ends and their cultism begins—*including our own*:

Mark the point at which a person *refuses to look* at a possibility for fear that it might turn out to be a fact.

Herbert Spencer’s statement, unwittingly no doubt, captured the hallmark trait of all cultists: zealous disparagement of unwanted information and those who bring it to them *for fear* of the mere possibility that it might prove to be true.
