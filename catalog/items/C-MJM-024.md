### Millard · 2025-06-03 16:45

The as-experienced artifacts of our interactions with real-world phenomena do not carry any built-in connection to what we experienced. We experienced something, yes, leaving us with experiential artifacts: the memories, impressions, and emotions resulting from an experience of certain real-world phenomena. We associate these artifacts to the real world phenomena we experienced--\*we\* do that--but no inherent guarantees exist that our associations correspond properly, accurately, or functionally to the phenomena we connect them to. It takes additional work completely apart from the experience itself--work that \*we\* must do--to ensure that the as-experienced phenomena in our heads resemble and actually refer to the real-world, as-is phenomena we associate them with.
What do you think about that?

### Claude · 2025-06-03 16:45

### Claude · 2025-06-03 16:49

A few significant criticisms come to mind:

**The verification problem**: If our experiential artifacts don't inherently connect to reality, then the "additional work" to verify correspondence faces the same problem. How do we know our verification processes themselves accurately connect to reality? This could lead to infinite regress - we'd need to verify our verification methods, then verify those verifications, and so on.

**Pragmatic objection**: While philosophically interesting, this view might be practically paralyzing. If we truly can't trust the basic correspondence between experience and reality, how do we function? Our ability to navigate the world suggests some reliable connection exists, even if imperfect.

**Evolutionary argument**: Natural selection would presumably favor organisms whose experiential artifacts correspond reasonably well to reality. Creatures whose internal representations were completely divorced from external phenomena wouldn't survive long. This suggests some built-in reliability, even if not perfect.

**The coherence challenge**: Your view seems to assume we can meaningfully distinguish between "experiential artifacts" and "real-world phenomena" - but if all our access to the world is through experience, this distinction might be incoherent. We might not be able to step outside experience to make this comparison.

**Degrees vs. binaries**: Perhaps the issue isn't that correspondence is absent and must be built from scratch, but that it exists in degrees and can be improved. This would make the problem more tractable without dismissing your core insight.

Which of these resonates as the strongest challenge to your framework?
