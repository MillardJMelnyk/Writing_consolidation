Is "ontologically real" a sensible, meaningful expression?

### Claude · 2025-07-13 16:27

Yes, "ontologically real" is a sensible and meaningful expression in philosophy, though it's somewhat redundant since ontology is precisely the study of what exists or what is real.

The phrase typically emphasizes that something exists independently of our minds, perceptions, or theories about it. Philosophers use it to distinguish between things that are merely conceptual, linguistic, or dependent on human cognition versus things that have genuine existence in reality.

For example, when debating whether numbers are "ontologically real," philosophers are asking whether mathematical objects exist independently in reality or are just useful human constructs. Similarly, discussions about whether consciousness, moral facts, or scientific entities like electrons are "ontologically real" concern whether these things exist as genuine features of reality rather than being mere products of human thought or social construction.

The expression is particularly common in metaphysics when philosophers want to stress the mind-independence of whatever they're discussing. While "real" alone might suffice, adding "ontologically" clarifies that we're talking about genuine existence rather than practical utility, social construction, or phenomenological reality.

So while it might seem redundant to some, "ontologically real" serves a useful disambiguating function in philosophical discourse where different senses of "real" are often in play.
