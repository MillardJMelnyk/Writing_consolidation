I was told to reduce my intake of rice, especially brown rice, due to arsenic. But the Japanese eat loads of rice!

### Claude · 2025-12-10 16:26

The tension dissolves with one fact: Japan predominantly eats **white rice**, not brown rice.

Brown rice retains the bran layer where arsenic accumulates. White rice has the bran removed, drastically reducing arsenic content. This is why the advice specifies brown rice.

Arsenic in rice is real—it accumulates in flooded paddies—but the dose and form matter. Japanese consumption of white rice hasn't produced documented mass arsenic poisoning, suggesting the exposure levels are tolerable or mitigated by other dietary factors.

If you prefer rice, white rice carries significantly lower arsenic burden than brown rice. The advice you received wasn't "avoid rice"—it was specifically about brown rice's higher arsenic concentration.

### Millard · 2025-12-10 16:28

what about arsenic and barley

### Claude · 2025-12-10 16:28

Barley accumulates far less arsenic than rice because of cultivation conditions, not the grain itself.

Rice grown in anaerobic paddy soil shows a dramatically higher shoot-to-soil arsenic ratio (~0.8) compared to wheat and barley (~0.2 and 0.1). Even when wheat and barley were grown in soils with over 200 μg/g arsenic, grain levels never exceeded 0.55 μg/g, whereas rice achieved such levels in soils with only 10 μg/g arsenic.

Barley grain contained arsenic at 0.2 mg/kg, which is at or near recommended guidelines and considered safe by FAO.

More directly: Rice purchased by shoppers contained 28 times more arsenic than nine alternative grains tested, including barley.

Barley is grown in aerobic (non-flooded) soil. That's the critical difference. Flooded rice paddies create anaerobic conditions where arsenic becomes more bioavailable and plants absorb it readily. Barley doesn't face this problem.

### Millard · 2025-12-10 16:30

are there other health/toxicity issues with barley?

### Claude · 2025-12-10 16:30

No major toxicity issues for the general population. Barley is non-toxic, but several specific concerns exist:

**Gluten**: Barley contains gluten. People with celiac disease or gluten sensitivity must avoid it entirely, including barley malt and flour.

**Mycotoxins**: Barley can be contaminated with fungal secondary metabolites including mycotoxins, though regulatory testing shows EU grain samples do not exceed permitted limits. Not a practical concern in commercial supplies meeting standards.

**Digestive side effects**: Gas, bloating, and unpleasant taste can occur; barley flour can sometimes cause asthma. High fiber intake can cause stomach cramps and constipation if introduced too rapidly.

**Sprouts**: Raw or lightly cooked barley sprouts carry bacterial risk; pregnant women, children, the elderly, and immunocompromised people should avoid them.

**Blood sugar interaction**: Barley might lower blood sugar levels; diabetes medications may need adjustment if combined with barley. Relevant only for diabetics on medication.

**Rare allergic reactions**: Barley allergies are possible; severe reactions to barley-containing beer have been documented in sensitive individuals.

Otherwise, barley is safe at normal consumption levels.

### Claude · 2025-12-10 16:32

Data exists only for **northwestern Mexico** (Sonora region), not specifically central Mexico.

A study of rice marketed in northwestern Mexico found total arsenic ranging from 0.06 to 0.29 mg/kg, with 40% of samples exceeding FAO/WHO recommended levels. However, when health risk was assessed by hazard quotient and carcinogenic risk, values did not exceed safety thresholds, and the rice supply in northwestern Mexico was determined to be safe for human consumption.

This applies to a region where arsenic contamination in groundwater is endemic—different from central Mexico's geography and hydrology.

The broader picture: Mexico is listed among countries with documented arsenic contamination in rice, alongside Bangladesh, China, India, and the USA. One analyst speculated that Mexican rice would likely be contaminated due to Mexico's widespread pesticide and fertilizer use, but noted this remains hypothesis rather than measured data.

Bottom line: No tested data for central Mexico (Querétaro). If concerned, apply the same rules as U.S. rice—white rice lower than brown, and where origin is known (California, India basmati, Thailand jasmine), choose those varieties. Rinsing and cooking rice in excess water (6:1 ratio, drain after) reduces arsenic by approximately 50%.

### Millard · 2025-12-10 16:36

how about lentils?

### Claude · 2025-12-10 16:37

Lentils consumed in the US had mean arsenic concentrations of 8.49±4.5, compared to rice.

The units in that study aren't specified in the abstract, so the direct numerical comparison isn't clear. However, context suggests lentils accumulate significantly less arsenic than rice: lentils are grown in aerobic soil (like barley), not flooded paddies, so bioavailability is lower.

Notably, Canadian lentils are being actively used as a therapeutic intervention for arsenic poisoning. Saskatchewan-grown lentils with high selenium content were tested in Bangladesh; participants consuming selenium-rich lentils had significantly higher urinary arsenic excretion and reduced symptoms of arsenic poisoning.

If lentils contained high arsenic themselves, they wouldn't be suitable for treating arsenic poisoning. The fact that they're used therapeutically to help people excrete absorbed arsenic (via selenium binding) indicates the lentil grain itself is not a major arsenic contamination concern.

Practically: lentils are a better choice than rice for arsenic exposure, particularly if you're eating Canadian or US-grown lentils.
